Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors is a standardized contractor trade examination accepted by participating licensing agencies, including the Arizona Registrar of Contractors.

The examination contains 115 questions. Candidates must answer at least 81 questions correctly to pass. The examination allows 330 minutes, or 5 hours and 30 minutes, for completion.

The examination evaluates knowledge across major areas of commercial building construction. Current examination content includes:

  • Planning and estimating
  • Financial and project management
  • Construction safety
  • Site construction
  • Concrete construction
  • Masonry
  • Metals
  • Wood foundations and framing
  • Thermal and moisture protection
  • Doors, windows, and glazing
  • Finishes
  • Specialty equipment and conveyance systems

These subjects require candidates to work with both practical construction knowledge and detailed technical references. Questions may involve calculations, construction procedures, management concepts, code requirements, safety regulations, materials, specifications, or standard industry practices.

The size of the exam makes time management important. Candidates should develop a strategy for answering questions they know, researching questions that require a reference, and avoiding excessive time spent searching for a single answer.

Open Book Test

The NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors is an open book test. Candidates may use permitted reference materials in accordance with the examination provider's rules.

Although the examination is open book, candidates should not expect to have enough time to look up every answer from scratch. With 115 questions and 330 minutes available, effective preparation should include repeated practice using the reference books.

Reference navigation is an important exam skill. Candidates should learn which publication addresses each major construction topic, become familiar with indexes and tables of contents, and practice finding specific regulations, tables, definitions, formulas, and construction requirements.

Open-book preparation is especially useful when working with references such as the International Building Code, OSHA regulations, ACI concrete standards, accessibility requirements, construction management books, and specialized trade manuals. Knowing which book to select before beginning a search can save valuable examination time.

Licensing Steps

The Arizona Registrar of Contractors, commonly referred to as AZ ROC, licenses contractors in Arizona. NASCLA examination acceptance can satisfy the applicable trade examination requirement for eligible Arizona building classifications, but applicants must complete the remainder of the Arizona licensing process.

  1. Determine the appropriate Arizona license classification. Arizona offers separate commercial, residential, and dual contractor classifications. The classification should match the scope of work the business intends to perform.
  2. Meet the experience requirements. Arizona requires a qualifying party with the experience, knowledge, and skills required for the applicable classification.
  3. Prepare for and pass the required examinations. For eligible B-1, B-2, KB-1, and KB-2 applicants, the NASCLA Commercial General Building examination may be used in place of the applicable AZ ROC trade examination.
  4. Complete Arizona's statutes and rules requirement. Arizona applicants must satisfy the state-specific examination or training requirement applicable to contractor licensing.
  5. Establish the licensing entity. A sole proprietorship, partnership, limited liability company, or corporation may apply. Corporations and LLCs must meet Arizona registration and good-standing requirements.
  6. Obtain the required contractor license bond. Arizona contractor license bond requirements vary according to license type and applicable business volume.
  7. Submit the contractor license application. Applicants must provide the Arizona Registrar of Contractors with the required application, qualifying-party information, supporting documents, and applicable fees.
  8. Receive approval before contracting. Contractors must hold the proper Arizona license before submitting bids or performing work that requires contractor licensing.

Candidates planning to use their NASCLA exam results in Arizona should make sure the classification they are pursuing accepts the NASCLA examination as an alternative to the Arizona trade exam.

State Requirements

Arizona contractor licensing is administered by the Arizona Registrar of Contractors. Arizona issues commercial, residential, and dual contractor licenses based on the type and scope of construction work performed.

The B-1 General Commercial Contractor classification allows qualifying commercial building construction, alteration, repair, management, and supervision within the scope established by AZ ROC. Certain specialty work, including electrical, plumbing, air conditioning systems, boilers, swimming pools, spas, and water wells, must be subcontracted to appropriately licensed contractors.

Arizona also offers the B-2 General Small Commercial Contractor classification for qualifying commercial construction projects subject to the scope and project-value limitations established for that classification.

The KB-1 Dual Building Contractor classification combines the applicable scope of the B-1 General Commercial Contractor and B General Residential Contractor classifications. The KB-2 Dual Residential and Small Commercial classification combines the applicable residential and small commercial scopes.

AZ ROC recognizes the NASCLA Commercial General Building examination as an alternative trade examination for eligible B-1, B-2, KB-1, and KB-2 applicants. The NASCLA exam does not replace Arizona's entire licensing process or create a national contractor license.

Applicants must also meet the experience requirements established for the requested classification. Arizona identifies four years of qualifying hands-on or managerial experience for its B-1 and B-2 general commercial classifications.
