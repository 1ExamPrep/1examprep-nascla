Arizona NASCLA Exam: Getting Your ROC License and Preparing to Pass

By Pass Your NASCLA Exam Prep · 12 min read · 9 official sources cited

Every contractor license in Arizona comes from one agency, the Arizona Registrar of Contractors, usually called the ROC. That keeps things simpler than in states with several boards. The ROC licenses commercial, residential and dual contractors under letter and number classifications such as B-1, C-11 and R-11, and each classification has its own experience and exam rules.

NASCLA exams are a real option here. The NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam can replace the Arizona trade exam for the B-1 and B-2 commercial classifications, and the NASCLA electrical exams can replace it for C-11, R-11 and CR-11. This page covers how those options work, what the ROC still requires, the published fees and bond amounts, and which of our study packages fits each exam. Orders are fulfilled by 1 Exam Prep.

Does Arizona Accept the NASCLA Exam?

Yes, for specific classifications. NASCLA's agency listing shows that the Arizona Registrar of Contractors began accepting the NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors on November 1, 2017. It is an option in place of the state trade exam for B-1 General Commercial Contractor and B-2 General Small Commercial Contractor. NASCLA also notes that the exam date on your transcript must fall within the past two years when you apply.

For electrical work, the ROC lets C-11, R-11 and CR-11 applicants take the NASCLA Electrical Examination Program instead of the Arizona trade exam. NASCLA's electrical agency list shows Arizona for the Electrical Contractor (Master Electrician) exam and the Residential Electrical Contractor exam. It does not show the journeyman exam for Arizona, which makes sense because the ROC licenses contracting businesses, not individual electricians.

What NASCLA does not cover: the Arizona Statutes and Rules Exam, the experience requirement, the bond, background checks and the rest of the application. Dual building classifications such as KB-1 and KB-2 are not on NASCLA's Arizona listing, so ask the ROC before you plan around a NASCLA score for those. You can check the current listings on NASCLA's Arizona agency page and the electrical participating agencies page.

Who Issues an Arizona Contractor License

The Arizona Registrar of Contractors issues and regulates contractor licenses statewide. Its main office is at 1700 W. Washington Street in Phoenix, and it takes applications through its Online Customer Portal, by mail or in person.

The ROC sorts licenses into three groups. Commercial licenses cover nonresidential work. Residential licenses cover homes. Dual licenses, which carry a K prefix for general work or a CR prefix for specialty work, cover both. Within each group there are general classifications, such as B-1 for general commercial building, and specialty classifications, such as the 11 series for electrical.

Each license is held by a business, whether a sole proprietor, partnership, LLC or corporation, and backed by a qualifying party. The qualifying party is the person who meets the experience requirement and passes the exams. That person must be regularly employed by the licensee and acts as its responsible managing employee. One person may qualify one additional entity only when the two share at least 25 percent common ownership or one is a subsidiary of the other.

Arizona License Classifications and the Exam Each One Needs

The ROC's License Classification Requirements form lists four years of experience, a trade exam and the Statutes and Rules Exam for each classification below. Each line notes where a NASCLA exam can stand in for the trade exam.

If your work crosses commercial and residential lines, compare the dual classifications before you test. Choosing the wrong one can mean another exam later.

  • B-1 General Commercial Contractor: NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam accepted in place of the trade exam.
  • B-2 General Small Commercial Contractor: NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam accepted in place of the trade exam.
  • KB-1 Dual Building Contractor and KB-2 Dual Residential and Small Commercial: trade exam required. NASCLA does not list these classifications for Arizona, so confirm with the ROC.
  • C-11 Electrical (commercial): NASCLA Electrical Contractor exam accepted in place of the trade exam.
  • CR-11 Electrical (dual residential and commercial): NASCLA Electrical Contractor exam accepted in place of the trade exam.
  • R-11 Electrical (residential): NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor exam accepted in place of the trade exam.

How to Get an Arizona Contractor License, Step by Step

The order below follows the ROC's own application checklist, with the NASCLA steps added where they fit. Keep copies of everything you submit.

  1. Pick the classification that matches your work and name a qualifying party with at least four years of hands-on or managerial experience in it.
  2. Form your business entity. LLCs and corporations register with the Arizona Corporation Commission, and partnerships register with the Arizona Secretary of State. Get an EIN from the IRS if you need one.
  3. Apply for the NASCLA exam at ned.nascla.org, get pre-approval, and schedule your test with PSI.
  4. Pass the NASCLA exam with at least 70 percent and keep your transcript, which must be dated within two years of your ROC application.
  5. Complete the Arizona Statutes and Rules Exam online through Gmetrix and pass it with at least 70 percent.
  6. Complete background checks for the business applicant and the qualifying party. The ROC treats a check as expired 90 days after completion.
  7. Buy a license bond from a surety licensed by the Arizona Department of Insurance in the amount your classification and volume require.
  8. Submit the application with your exam results, background check receipts, entity papers, bond, ID and fees.
  9. Answer any deficiency letter quickly. Time spent waiting on your reply does not count toward the ROC's review clock.

Inside the Arizona NASCLA Exam: Format and Rules

The NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam has 115 scored questions and a 330 minute time limit, and it may include unscored pretest items. You need 70 percent to pass, which works out to 81 correct answers. It is open book. You bring the approved references, which may be highlighted, underlined and indexed before test day, with permanent tabs only. Removable tabs, loose notes and writing in the books during the exam are not allowed, and you can use a silent, nonprinting, non-programmable calculator.

Expect a wide spread of topics. Project management, contracts, estimating and scheduling carry a lot of weight, alongside concrete, masonry, steel, wood framing, roofing, gypsum, sitework, accessibility, the International Building Code and OSHA safety. The challenge is less about whether the answer is in your books and more about finding it fast enough.

The NASCLA electrical exams are also open book. The contractor exam centers on the National Electrical Code along with safety, theory, calculations, energy standards and project management. The residential exam adds topics such as fire alarm systems and residential code. Our Arizona C-11 packages use the 2020 NEC, while our R-11 packages use the 2023 NEC, because the approved reference lists differ. Always confirm the current edition in NASCLA's candidate bulletin and reference list before you buy or tab books.

The ROC says trade exams, including the NASCLA options, are given through PSI, either online or at a test center. PSI's Arizona contractor line is 855-744-0310.

The Arizona Statutes and Rules Exam and Business Knowledge

Every qualifying party takes the Arizona Statutes and Rules Exam, often shortened to SRE. The ROC describes it as a computer based course and exam offered only online through Gmetrix, and the passing standard is 70 percent, the same as other ROC exams. As the name says, it focuses on Arizona contractor statutes and ROC rules rather than trade skills. A NASCLA score does not replace it.

The ROC's application page says the SRE cannot be waived. Its classification form mentions a waiver for a person who was a qualifying party on an Arizona license within the past five years. If you think that applies to you, ask the ROC directly before you skip it.

For business law study, NASCLA publishes an Arizona edition of its Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, now in its 7th Edition on NASCLA's bookstore. It covers business setup, contracts, estimating, risk, employment rules, taxes and lien law with an Arizona focus. It makes a solid companion to the SRE course and to running your company once you are licensed. If you plan to use it as a reference during any exam, check that exam's rules first.

Arizona Experience, Bond and Recovery Fund Requirements

Each classification covered on this page requires four years of experience for the qualifying party. The ROC's form says that experience must be hands-on or managerial. You document it as part of the application, so collect employer names, dates and job descriptions early.

Every license needs a bond. For commercial general contractors, which includes B-1 and B-2, the published bond amounts run from $5,000 for annual volume of $150,000 or less up to $100,000 for volume over $10 million. Commercial specialty contractors such as C-11 range from $2,500 to $50,000 on the same volume brackets. Residential specialty contractors such as R-11 post $4,250 under $375,000 in volume or $7,500 at $375,000 or more. Residential general contractors post $9,000 under $750,000 or $15,000 above it. Dual licenses combine the residential and commercial amounts.

Residential and dual licensees also pay an assessment into the ROC's residential recovery fund, or post a separate $200,000 surety bond or cash deposit instead. Commercial only licenses, such as B-1, B-2 and C-11, have no recovery fund assessment.

The ROC also runs background checks on the applicant and qualifying party, and everyone listed on the application must be at least 18.

Arizona Contractor License Fees

These are the ROC's published fees for a two year license. They do not include exam fees, and they can change, so check the ROC fee page before you apply. Your bond premium, background checks and study materials are separate costs.

  • General Commercial (A, B-1, B-2): $200 application plus $580 license, $780 total. Renewal: $580.
  • Specialty Commercial (C, including C-11): $100 application plus $480 license, $580 total. Renewal: $480.
  • Specialty Residential (R, including R-11): $80 application, $270 license and $370 recovery fund, $720 total. Renewal: $540.
  • Specialty Dual (CR, including CR-11): $100 application, $380 license and $370 recovery fund, $850 total. Renewal: $650.
  • General Dual (KA, KB-1, KB-2): $200 application, $480 license and $370 recovery fund, $1,050 total.
  • NASCLA Commercial General Building exam: $106 exam fee to PSI plus a $25 application fee to NASCLA.
  • NASCLA Electrical exam: $116 paid to PSI.

Trade Exam Waivers and Licenses From Other States

Arizona can waive the trade exam under rule R4-9-106(E) if your qualifying party served as qualifying party, within the past five years, on a license in good standing in the same Arizona classification or a comparable classification in another state. You file the ROC's Application to Waive a Trade Examination in place of trade exam results. The SRE still applies.

Arizona also has a universal licensing law, A.R.S. 32-4302, for people licensed in another state for at least 12 months in the same discipline and practice level, subject to other conditions in that law. Ask the ROC how it applies to your classification.

If you work in several states, a NASCLA score is often the more portable choice. It sits in NASCLA's National Examination Database, and you can send it to any participating agency that accepts that exam. Keep the two year transcript window in mind for Arizona, and plan your ROC application soon after you pass.

After You Pass: Keeping Your Arizona License Active

Once the ROC approves your application, you get a mailed notice and a call from a licensing representative. Arizona licenses run for two years, and you renew at the fees shown above. Residential and dual licensees pay the recovery fund assessment again at renewal, $270 on the current schedule.

LLCs and corporations must stay in good standing with the Arizona Corporation Commission, both to apply and to renew. Keep your bond active and sized to your current annual volume. If your business grows into a higher bracket, raise the bond to match.

Your qualifying party matters after licensing too. If that person leaves, the company needs a replacement who meets the experience and exam rules for the classification. If you add a classification later, check whether another NASCLA exam can cover the trade portion before you sign up for a state trade test. A quick call to the ROC can save you a wasted exam fee.

Arizona NASCLA exam and license FAQ

Does Arizona accept the NASCLA commercial exam?

Yes. Since November 1, 2017, the Arizona Registrar of Contractors has accepted the NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam in place of the state trade exam for B-1 and B-2. Your transcript must show an exam date within the past two years.

Can I use a NASCLA exam for an Arizona electrical license?

Yes. The ROC accepts the NASCLA Electrical Examination Program instead of the Arizona trade exam for C-11, R-11 and CR-11. The contractor exam fits C-11 and CR-11, and the residential exam fits R-11.

Do I still need the Statutes and Rules Exam if I pass NASCLA?

Yes. The SRE covers Arizona contractor law and is separate from the trade exam. It is taken online through Gmetrix, and a NASCLA score does not replace it.

How much experience do I need for a B-1 license in Arizona?

The ROC's classification form lists four years of hands-on or managerial experience for the qualifying party on B-1. The same four years applies to B-2, KB-1, KB-2, C-11, R-11 and CR-11.

How much does an Arizona B-1 license cost?

The ROC lists a $200 application fee and a $580 license fee, $780 total, for a two year General Commercial license. Exam fees and your bond premium are extra. Confirm current figures on the ROC fee page.

What bond do I need for an Arizona commercial license?

It depends on your classification and expected annual volume. Commercial general contractors range from $5,000 at $150,000 or less up to $100,000 above $10 million, according to the ROC's bond page.

What does the NASCLA exam cost in Arizona?

The ROC lists $106 paid to PSI plus a $25 application fee to NASCLA for the commercial general building exam. The NASCLA electrical exam is listed at $116 paid to PSI.

Can Arizona waive my trade exam?

Possibly. If your qualifying party qualified a license in good standing in the same or a comparable classification within the past five years, in Arizona or another state, you can apply for a trade exam waiver. The SRE is still required.

How long is an Arizona contractor license good for?

The ROC's published license fees are for a two year license. You renew before it expires, and residential and dual licensees pay the recovery fund assessment again at renewal.
