## Open Book Test

The NASCLA Accredited Trade Examination for Residential Electrical Contractors is an open book test. Candidates are responsible for bringing their own approved references to the examination center. The examination center does not provide the reference books, although a physical diagram or blueprint packet may be handed out onsite at the testing center when required for the exam.

Approved reference materials may be highlighted, underlined, annotated, and indexed before the exam session begins. Candidates may not write, highlight, underline, or index inside the references during the examination session. Candidates are not permitted to bring additional papers, whether loose or attached, with their approved references.

References may be tabbed or indexed with permanent tabs only. Permanent tabs are tabs that would tear the page if removed. Temporary tabs, including removable sticky notes or other removable tabs, are not allowed and must be removed before the exam begins.

If a candidate downloads a permitted reference from the internet, the material must be bound before it can be brought into the testing center. Spiral binding or a hole-punched binder format may be used when allowed by the exam instructions. Candidates may use a silent, nonprinting, non-programmable calculator in the examination center.

Open book does not mean easy. This exam rewards candidates who know the material and can use the references quickly. The NEC, OSHA standards, NASCLA contractor guide, NFPA 70E, Ugly’s Electrical References, NFPA 72, electrical theory reference, ANSI safety labeling standard, and IRC all cover different types of information. Candidates should study with the books before test day, build familiarity with tables and indexes, and practice finding answers under time limits.

## Test Information and Study Materials

The NASCLA residential electrical contractor exam is broad, detailed, and reference-based. Candidates should study the NEC consistently and learn how to use the index, tables, chapters, definitions, and article structure. Wiring Methods and Practices, Circuit Protection and Installation, Grounding and Bonding, and Equipment for General Use make up a large portion of the exam, so NEC navigation is one of the most important skills to develop.

Theory preparation should include Ohm’s law, power formulas, voltage, current, resistance, conductor properties, circuit behavior, voltage drop, grounding concepts, transformers, motors, and electrical calculations. Candidates should be comfortable applying theory to real code situations rather than only memorizing formulas.

Personal Jobsite Safety preparation should include OSHA construction standards and NFPA 70E safety-related work practices. Students should review electrical hazards, shock protection, arc flash awareness, energized work practices, personal protective equipment, lockout-related safety concepts, safe tool use, jobsite hazards, safety signs, and construction safety responsibilities.

Project Design, Estimating, and Measurement preparation should include estimating awareness, plan reading, material quantities, measurements, documentation, communication, project coordination, and business-related contractor responsibilities. Electrical contractors are often responsible for more than installation work. They also need to understand planning, supervision, documentation, and project organization.

Grounding and Bonding preparation should include grounding electrode systems, equipment grounding conductors, bonding requirements, grounded conductors, service bonding, fault-current paths, grounding connections, and NEC requirements. This is one of the largest exam sections, so candidates should give it consistent study time.

Circuit Protection and Installation preparation should include overcurrent protection, branch circuits, feeders, services, conductor sizing, panelboards, disconnects, load awareness, protection requirements, and installation procedures. Wiring Methods and Practices preparation should include raceways, cables, boxes, fittings, conductors, supports, terminations, bends, fill, protection from damage, and residential installation practices.

Special System and Special Equipment Installations may include fire alarm and signaling concepts, special equipment, residential electrical systems, and code requirements that go beyond basic branch circuit installation. Candidates should review NFPA 72 and the NEC sections that apply to special systems and equipment.

Tools and Equipment preparation should include safe tool use, test instruments, hand tools, power tools, meters, installation equipment, and jobsite equipment awareness. Equipment for General Use may include switches, receptacles, luminaires, panelboards, motors, appliances, transformers, disconnects, and general electrical equipment installation rules.

Energy Efficiency/Management is a small section, but candidates should still review the IRC and energy-related requirements connected to residential electrical systems. Since the exam contains only one item in this area, preparation should be efficient but not ignored.
