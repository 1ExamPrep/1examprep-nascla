Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program offers a standardized trade examination for residential electrical contractors. The residential examination is intended for professionals who demonstrate competency in electrical installation, service, repair, and maintenance work typically encountered in permanent dwelling units.

Arizona applicants pursuing the R-11 Electrical Residential classification may use the NASCLA Accredited Residential Electrical Contractor Examination instead of the Arizona-specific trade examination. The qualifying party must still complete the remaining requirements connected with the Arizona contractor license application.

The qualifying party is the individual whose experience and examination qualifications support the contractor license. This person must demonstrate the applicable electrical background and remain associated with the licensed business in the capacity recognized by the Arizona Registrar of Contractors.

The examination preparation program may address electrical theory, branch circuits, feeders, services, conductor sizing, wiring methods, grounding, bonding, overcurrent protection, dwelling-unit calculations, motors, equipment, fire alarm systems, workplace safety, residential construction, labeling, and contractor business practices.

Questions may require candidates to interpret National Electrical Code provisions, complete calculations, identify proper installation methods, select suitable materials, recognize electrical hazards, or determine how residential construction requirements affect an electrical installation.

Fire alarm preparation may involve initiating devices, notification appliances, circuits, system documentation, testing, inspection, maintenance, and residential alarm requirements. Candidates should learn when a question belongs in NFPA 72 rather than the National Electrical Code.

The International Residential Code supports study of one- and two-family dwelling construction. Electrical contractors benefit from understanding residential building assemblies, wall and ceiling spaces, openings, penetrations, fireblocking, access, structural components, and other conditions that influence electrical installations.

The NASCLA business, law, and project management reference supports preparation involving business organization, estimating, contracts, scheduling, financial management, insurance, safety, workforce responsibilities, and project administration.

Passing the NASCLA examination does not create a national electrical license. It provides an accredited trade-exam result that may be accepted by participating licensing agencies. The applicant must still apply for and receive the Arizona R-11 contractor license before offering or performing regulated contracting work in Arizona.

Open Book Test

The NASCLA Accredited Residential Electrical Contractor Examination is an open-book test using approved reference materials. Candidates may consult permitted books during the examination, subject to the testing provider’s current rules regarding editions, tabs, annotations, markings, attachments, and prohibited materials.

An open-book format does not eliminate the need for thorough preparation. The examination library covers electrical codes, safety standards, residential construction, fire alarms, product labeling, electrical theory, calculations, project management, and contractor business practices.

Candidates should know which reference is most likely to contain the answer before beginning a search. A question involving conductor ampacity, grounding, services, or branch circuits may require the National Electrical Code. A jobsite safety question may require OSHA, while an energized-work question may require NFPA 70E.

Licensing Steps

  1. Confirm the R-11 classification. Review the Arizona Electrical Residential scope and make sure it matches the residential electrical services the business intends to offer.
  2. Select the qualifying party. Identify the individual who will satisfy the applicable electrical experience and examination requirements.
  3. Choose the NASCLA exam option. Use the NASCLA Accredited Residential Electrical Contractor Examination in place of the applicable Arizona trade exam.
  4. Prepare for the examination. Use the included course, highlighted and tabbed books, code-navigation drills, theory review, and calculation practice.
  5. Complete the NASCLA examination process. Register for and pass the accredited residential electrical contractor examination.
  6. Complete Arizona’s additional examination requirement. Complete the Arizona Statutes and Rules requirement unless an authorized waiver applies.
  7. Form the business entity. Use the included Business Formation service to establish an LLC or corporation for the electrical contracting business.
  8. Obtain an EIN. Complete the included EIN Filing with the IRS for banking, taxation, payroll, hiring, and professional operations.
  9. Complete applicable background requirements. Follow Arizona’s process for the individuals required to appear on the contractor license application.
  10. Prepare the contractor application. Organize qualifying-party records, experience documentation, exam information, business documents, and required disclosures.
  11. Obtain the required contractor bond. Arrange the bond applicable to the residential electrical classification and anticipated volume of work.
  12. Address residential financial protection. Complete the applicable Residential Contractors’ Recovery Fund requirement or authorized alternative.
  13. Submit the application. Provide the completed application and supporting materials to the Arizona Registrar of Contractors.
  14. Wait for license issuance. Do not advertise, contract for, or perform work requiring the R-11 license until Arizona approves the application and issues an active contractor license.

State Requirements

The Arizona R-11 classification applies to residential electrical contracting within the scope established by the Arizona Registrar of Contractors. Contractors planning to perform commercial electrical work may need a commercial or dual electrical classification.

The proposed qualifying party must demonstrate the experience, knowledge, and skills required for the R-11 license. Experience records may need to describe residential wiring, service work, equipment installation, troubleshooting, supervision, and the duration of relevant electrical experience.

Arizona accepts the NASCLA Accredited Residential Electrical Contractor Examination as an alternative to the applicable state trade exam for R-11 applicants. The examination result satisfies the trade-exam component but does not replace the remaining licensing requirements.

The legal business entity applying for the contractor license must be properly formed and identified. The company name, ownership records, qualifying-party information, EIN documents, contractor bond, and license application should remain consistent throughout the process.

Arizona contractor applicants must complete applicable background requirements and provide the identification, experience, examination, business, and disclosure information required for state review.

A contractor license bond is required. The applicable bond amount depends on the residential license category and anticipated annual gross volume of work. Residential contractors must also satisfy Arizona’s residential financial-protection requirements.

Passing the examination does not authorize the applicant to operate as a licensed contractor. The Arizona Registrar of Contractors must review and approve the complete application before the R-11 license is issued.

After licensing, the contractor must operate within the authorized classification, maintain the required bond, renew the license, and keep business and qualifying-party information current. Changes involving ownership, business structure, address, bond, or qualifying party may require additional filings.

Test Information and Study Materials

Effective preparation should combine National Electrical Code navigation, electrical theory, calculations, residential construction, fire alarm systems, OSHA safety, NFPA 70E safe-work practices, and contractor business knowledge.

NEC study may include dwelling-unit branch circuits, feeders, services, conductor ampacity, wiring methods, overcurrent protection, grounding, bonding, boxes, raceways, cables, appliances, motors, generators, equipment, and special systems.

Calculation practice may involve dwelling loads, services, feeders, voltage drop, conductor sizing, box fill, raceway fill, motors, grounding conductors, and overcurrent protection. Candidates should practice selecting the correct code table or formula before beginning the calculation.

Electrical theory preparation may include voltage, current, resistance, power, series circuits, parallel circuits, alternating current, impedance, transformers, motors, and the relationship between theory and code requirements.

Safety preparation should distinguish among OSHA construction requirements and NFPA 70E electrical safe-work practices. Candidates should understand jobsite hazards, energized equipment, approach boundaries, protective equipment, and procedures for reducing electrical risk.

Fire alarm review may include alarm terminology, initiating devices, notification appliances, circuits, inspection, testing, and documentation. Residential code study may include building assemblies, penetrations, fireblocking, structural components, and access conditions that affect electrical work.

A practical study schedule can combine topic review, short lookup drills, calculations, theory questions, safety questions, and longer timed practice sessions. Candidates should complete direct questions first and return to items requiring extended research after answering faster questions.
