## Does California accept the NASCLA exam?

Yes, but only through a narrow waiver. At its March 2025 meeting, CSLB approved the NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors for reciprocity purposes, and announced it in an industry bulletin dated April 25, 2025. NASCLA's agency list now shows California as a participating agency with the note that the B General Building trade exam may be waived for applicants coming from out of state.

The conditions are strict. You must hold a general building contractor license (or a license with a similar title) that has been in good standing in another state for the previous five years, and you must have passed the NASCLA commercial exam. NASCLA's listing also says that California residents must take the California trade exam, so this path is built for contractors licensed elsewhere who are expanding into California.

The waiver covers only the B trade exam. You still take the CSLB Law and Business exam, and you still meet California's bond, fingerprinting, workers' compensation and application rules. CSLB decides each waiver request, and its bulletin says staff review whether the other state's licensing standards are comparable. If you do not meet the conditions, you take the regular CSLB trade exam for the classification you want.

## Who issues contractor licenses in California

The [Contractors State License Board](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/), or CSLB, licenses contractors across California. It sets the classifications, reviews applications and experience, runs background checks through fingerprinting, schedules exams through its testing vendor, and enforces license law.

California's licensing threshold is low compared with most states. You generally need a CSLB license before you bid or perform construction work when labor and materials on a project total more than the minor work limit. Under Assembly Bill 2622, that limit rose from $500 to $1,000 on January 1, 2025, and the exemption only applies when the job needs no building permit and the unlicensed person uses no workers. For the commercial work NASCLA candidates usually pursue, you will need a license.

A state license does not replace city or county business licenses, permits, plan approvals or inspections. Plan on handling those locally for each project.

## California license types and which exam each needs

CSLB issues licenses in many classifications, but the NASCLA waiver ties to just one: the B General Building contractor. CSLB describes a B contractor's principal business as work on a structure that needs at least two unrelated building trades or crafts. Framing and carpentry can be performed by a B contractor without that limit. The B license is the classic general contractor license for building projects, from tenant improvements to new commercial structures.

Here is how exams line up for an out-of-state applicant.

CSLB also has a separate, older reciprocity program for contractors licensed in Arizona, Louisiana, Mississippi, Nevada and North Carolina. That program covers specific classifications on a reciprocal classifications list. The NASCLA route is different because it is open to general building licensees from any state.

- B General Building, out-of-state applicant licensed five years who passed NASCLA: trade exam can be waived; CSLB Law and Business exam still required.
- B General Building, California resident: the California trade exam is required, according to NASCLA's listing for California.
- Other classifications (C specialty classes, A General Engineering): the NASCLA waiver does not apply; use the regular CSLB exams or the five-state reciprocity list if you qualify.

## How to get a California B General Building license with NASCLA, step by step

Use this order as your checklist. CSLB's forms and the NASCLA site have the final word on every step.

1. Confirm you have held a general building contractor license in good standing in another state for the previous five years.
2. Apply for the NASCLA commercial exam at ned.nascla.org and wait for pre-approval.
3. Schedule with PSI using the candidate ID NASCLA sends, then pass the exam.
4. Log into your NASCLA account and request that your result be shared with California.
5. Complete CSLB's Application for Original Contractor's License for the B classification, with the $450 application fee.
6. Include a signed and dated statement asking CSLB to waive the B trade exam based on your NASCLA pass.
7. Send the Request for Verification of License form to the agency that issued your current license so it can complete and return it.
8. Get fingerprinted once CSLB accepts your application, and complete any experience documentation CSLB asks for.
9. Schedule and pass the CSLB Law and Business exam with PSI.
10. When CSLB sends your bond and fee letter, file the $25,000 contractor bond, proof of workers' compensation or an exemption, the asbestos open-book exam, and the initial license fee.

## The NASCLA commercial exam you will take

The NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors has 115 scored questions and 330 minutes on the clock, which is five and a half hours. You need 70 percent to pass, meaning 81 correct answers. PSI may include up to 10 unscored pretest questions, and the time limit accounts for them.

The test is open book, and you supply the approved references. PSI allows highlighting, underlining and permanent tabs added before the exam. Removable tabs, sticky notes and loose papers are not allowed. The exam spans general requirements, procurement and contracting, project and jobsite management, site work, concrete, masonry, metals, wood, moisture protection, doors and windows, finishes, accessibility, mechanical, plumbing, electrical and safety. With that much ground, knowing which book holds which answer is half the battle.

You apply at ned.nascla.org. NASCLA publishes a $65 application fee and says it usually processes applications within seven business days. An approval is good for one year with up to three attempts. Afterward, PSI emails a candidate ID and scheduling link, and you can test at any PSI location that offers the exam, not just one in California. When you pass, PSI gives you a pass report, and you buy a transcript for each agency you want to receive it. NASCLA lists $45 per agency, and results themselves do not expire.

## The California Law and Business exam

Every waiver applicant still has to pass the CSLB Law and Business exam. It is California's own test of the business and legal side of contracting in the state. We do not sell a California Law and Business product, but CSLB publishes a free [Law and Business study guide](https://cslb.ca.gov/Resources/StudyGuides/LawStudyGuide.pdf) that is a good place to start.

PSI administers CSLB exams on computer in a multiple-choice format. CSLB's exam FAQ lists a time limit of three and a half hours. Unlike the NASCLA test, you cannot bring books or notes into the room. A Spanish version is available, and CSLB says the Law and Business exam can be taken at PSI sites in several other states, which helps if you live outside California.

After CSLB accepts your application, it sends a Notice to Schedule, and you book and pay through PSI by phone at (877) 392-6422 or online. You have 18 months from acceptance to pass, with unlimited attempts, but you must wait 21 calendar days after a failed attempt before retaking it. Results print at the end of your session. A passing report does not show a score and is not a license; CSLB mails a bond and fee letter with the final steps.

## California B General Building license requirements: experience, bond and insurance

CSLB's standard rule calls for a qualifying individual who is at least 18 and has at least four years of journey-level experience in the classification within the last ten years. Time as a journeyman, foreman, supervising employee or contractor can count. CSLB may randomly pick applicants to document their experience, so keep pay records and project lists handy. The 2025 bulletin says waiver applicants must still meet California's other licensing requirements, so expect your experience to be reviewed.

Every applicant is fingerprinted at their own expense and must clear a criminal background check before the license issues. At issuance you file a $25,000 contractor bond. If a responsible managing employee or officer qualifies the license, a separate $25,000 bond of qualifying individual may apply, though CSLB can waive it when the qualifier owns at least 10 percent of the company. Limited liability companies have added bond and liability insurance rules.

You must also show workers' compensation coverage or file an exemption if you have no employees. CSLB notes that exemptions are not allowed when a responsible managing employee qualifies the license. Finally, every new licensee completes a short open-book asbestos exam before the license can be issued.

## California contractor license fees

These are the published figures we found on CSLB and NASCLA pages. Check both sites before paying, because fees change.

- CSLB application fee for an original license (exam or waiver, one classification): $450.
- CSLB initial license fee: $200 for a sole owner or $350 for a non-sole owner, covering two years.
- CSLB active renewal on time: $450 for a sole owner or $700 for a non-sole owner.
- NASCLA exam application: $65.
- NASCLA transcript sent to CSLB: $45.
- PSI exam fees for CSLB tests are paid to PSI; CSLB's FAQ does not list the amount, so check PSI when you schedule.
- Fingerprinting: varies by Live Scan location.

## After you are licensed: renewal and staying in good standing

CSLB licenses run for two years. Your initial license fee covers the first two years, and you renew on time to avoid higher delinquent fees, which CSLB lists at $675 for a sole owner and $1,050 for a non-sole owner. Keep your bond, workers' compensation coverage and qualifier current the whole time, and check CSLB's site for what happens if any of them lapse.

If you add classifications later, those go through CSLB's own process. The NASCLA waiver is tied only to the B General Building classification. Your NASCLA result does not expire, so you can also send transcripts to other participating states as your business grows.

## California NASCLA exam and license FAQ

### Does California accept the NASCLA exam?

Yes, as a waiver of the B General Building trade exam for out-of-state applicants. You must have passed NASCLA and held a general building license in good standing in another state for the previous five years.

### Can a California resident use NASCLA to skip the trade exam?

NASCLA's listing for California says residents must take the California trade exam. The waiver is meant for contractors applying from out of state.

### Do I still take the CSLB Law and Business exam with a NASCLA waiver?

Yes. CSLB's 2025 bulletin says waiver applicants must still pass the Law and Business exam. NASCLA covers only the trade portion.

### Which CSLB classification does the NASCLA waiver cover?

Only the B General Building classification. For A General Engineering or C specialty licenses, you follow CSLB's regular exam rules or the five-state reciprocity list.

### When did California start accepting NASCLA?

CSLB approved the NASCLA commercial exam for reciprocity at its March 2025 meeting and announced it in a bulletin dated April 25, 2025.

### How much does it cost to apply for a California contractor license?

CSLB's published application fee is $450, and the initial license fee is $200 for a sole owner or $350 for other business types. Add the bond premium, fingerprinting, PSI exam fees and NASCLA's $65 application and $45 transcript fees.

### What bond does California require?

CSLB requires a $25,000 contractor bond before the license issues. A $25,000 bond of qualifying individual may also apply when the qualifier is an employee or officer.

### Is the NASCLA exam open book?

Yes. You bring the approved references, which may be highlighted and permanently tabbed before the exam. The CSLB Law and Business exam, by contrast, does not allow books in the room.

### Can I take the NASCLA exam outside California?

Yes. NASCLA says you can test at any PSI site that offers the exam, no matter which state you plan to license in.
