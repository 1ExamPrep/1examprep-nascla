## Does Connecticut accept the NASCLA exam?

Not for trade licensing, as far as the published lists show. NASCLA keeps a [list of agencies that accept its commercial general building exam](https://www.nascla.org/page/ParticipatingAgencies), and Connecticut is not on it. Connecticut is also absent from NASCLA's [electrical exam participating agencies](https://www.nascla.org/page/ParticipatingAgencies_Electrical). That means passing a NASCLA trade exam elsewhere does not, by itself, stand in for a Connecticut trade exam.

Where NASCLA does matter is the business side. NASCLA lists the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection among the states with a state business and law exam, and DCP names the Connecticut edition of the NASCLA guide as the [required text for the state contractor examination](https://portal.ct.gov/dcp/occupational-and-professional-division/occupational--profess/about-careers-in-the-trades). NASCLA publishes the book; it does not run or grade the Connecticut test.

So for most readers the real question is not whether NASCLA replaces anything. It is how to pass the Business and Law portion the first time, using the right edition. If you hold a license from another state and hope to skip Connecticut testing, contact DCP before you spend money on any exam, because the answer depends on your trade and history.

## Who issues contractor licenses in Connecticut

The [Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection](https://portal.ct.gov/dcp/occupational-and-professional-division/occupational--profess/about-careers-in-the-trades) (DCP) handles construction credentials. Its Occupational and Professional Licensing Division issues trade licenses for work such as electrical, plumbing, heating, piping and cooling, sheet metal and similar fields. DCP sets the amount and type of experience and training each license requires, and PSI administers the licensing exams under a contract with the department.

Connecticut has no single general contractor license. Instead, DCP runs several separate programs. Contractors who work for homeowners on existing homes register under the [home improvement program](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/License-Services-Division/All-License-Applications/Home-Improvement-Applications). Builders of new homes register under the [new home construction program](https://portal.ct.gov/dcp/trade-practices-division/frequently-asked-questions-about-new-home-construction-registration). A separate Major Contractor registration covers larger commercial building work, and its application asks for a [credit reference](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/DCP/occpro/forms/MCO_IndustryRef.pdf). The home improvement and new home programs, based on DCP's published material, do not list a trade exam.

Because the programs overlap, start by writing down the kinds of jobs you plan to take. That list tells you which registration or license you need, and whether an exam is part of it.

## Connecticut contractor license types and which exam each needs

Connecticut trade licenses come in pairs: a contractor license and a journeyperson license. Journeypersons work for a licensed contractor. The contractor license is the one that lets you run the business, and it is the one that carries the Business and Law requirement.

PSI's [Connecticut candidate bulletin](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/49) for the electrical trades states that every contractor and dealer candidate must pass a Business and Law exam and a trade exam, while journeyperson candidates take only the trade portion. DCP's general guidance points all state contractor exam candidates to the Connecticut NASCLA guide. Check the bulletin for your own trade to confirm.

Examples of contractor-level licenses you will see on DCP's pages include:

- E-1 Unlimited Electrical Contractor, plus limited electrical contractor licenses such as C-5, L-5, T-1, L-1 lines and PV-1 solar ([DCP electrical license types](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/Occupational-and-Professional-Division/Occupational--Profess/Electrical-License-Types-and-Scope-of-Work))
- P-1 Unlimited Plumbing Contractor and limited plumbing, well, sprinkler, process piping and pool contractor licenses such as J-1, J-3, P-7, P-9, PP-1 and SP-1 ([DCP plumbing licenses](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/Occupational-and-Professional-Division/Occupational--Profess/Plumbing-Licenses-and-Scope-of-Work))
- Heating, piping and cooling, sheet metal and related contractor licenses such as S-1 and SM-1 ([DCP heating and cooling applications](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/License-Services-Division/All-License-Applications/Heating-Cooling-and-Sheet-Metal-Work-Licenses))
- Home improvement and new home construction registrations, which do not show a Business and Law exam in DCP's published material

## How to get a Connecticut contractor license, step by step

Here is the usual path for a trade contractor license. Your trade may add steps, so read the DCP application page for your license before you begin.

1. Pick the exact license that matches your work, such as E-1, P-1 or S-1, and read its scope of work on the DCP site.
2. Confirm you meet the experience requirement. For many contractor licenses that means two years holding the matching journeyperson license, or an equivalent experience and training path.
3. Apply to DCP online through eLicense or on the paper form. Only individuals can apply for a trade license, not companies.
4. Pay the nonrefundable application fee. DCP does not pre-review applications, so make sure your documents are complete.
5. Wait for DCP to approve you for testing. Eligibility is set by the state and, per PSI, lasts one year from approval.
6. Register and pay for each exam portion with PSI online or by phone, then pick a test center and date.
7. Study the Connecticut 6th Edition guide for Business and Law and the code books for your trade exam.
8. Pass both portions. Each passed portion is good for two years, so plan to finish both inside that window.
9. Send the license fee listed on your passing score report to DCP to have the license issued.

## The Connecticut Business and Law exam

According to PSI's [Connecticut bulletin](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/49), updated in February 2026, the Business and Law exam has 50 questions, a two hour time limit and a passing score of 70 percent. It is open book, and the listed reference is the Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Connecticut 6th Edition, published by NASCLA. NASCLA's bookstore lists the [Connecticut 6th Edition](https://store.nascla.org/collections/connecticut) as the current Connecticut title.

The published content outline spreads the questions across eleven areas. Estimating and bidding carries the most weight at 7 questions, followed by contracts at 6. Licensing, tax laws, labor laws, project management and environmental and safety each have 5. Financial management and risk management have 4 each, and lien law and business organization have 2 each.

Book rules matter on an open book test. PSI allows permanent tabs only. Temporary tabs such as sticky notes are not allowed and must come out before the exam starts, and you may not write in your references during the session. The proctor makes the final call on whether a book is acceptable, so make sure every tab is firmly attached.

With 50 questions in two hours you have a little over two minutes per question. That is enough time to look things up, but only if you already know where each topic lives in the book.

## The trade exam, PSI scheduling and retakes

Your trade exam is separate from Business and Law and uses its own code references, which PSI lists in the bulletin for your trade. PSI schedules Connecticut candidates [online or by phone](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/49) and lists test centers in West Hartford and Milford, along with several Massachusetts sites.

Contractor candidates may retake exams on an unlimited basis under the current bulletin, but you cannot book a new attempt on the same day you tested. If you fail, PSI gives you 30 minutes at the end of the session to review the items you missed. A passed portion stays valid for two years, and your exam eligibility lasts one year from state approval, so a failed attempt is a setback in time more than in standing.

Bring the government-issued ID PSI asks for and arrive early. Read the current bulletin a week before your date, since rules on references and check-in can change.

## Experience requirements

DCP publishes the experience rules by license. For the [electrical contractor licenses](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/Occupational-and-Professional-Division/Occupational--Profess/Electrical-License-Types-and-Scope-of-Work) such as E-1, C-5, L-1 and L-5, the standard is two years as a licensed journeyperson or six years of equivalent experience and training. Journeyperson licenses generally call for a registered apprenticeship or four years of equivalent experience and training.

The [plumbing contractor licenses](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/Occupational-and-Professional-Division/Occupational--Profess/Plumbing-Licenses-and-Scope-of-Work) such as P-1 follow the same idea: two years as a journeyperson in the matching category, or an equivalent DCP accepts. On the heating side, the [equivalent experience path](https://portal.ct.gov/dcp/license-services-division/all-license-applications/heating-contractor---equivalent-experience-and-training) is strict. For S-1, DCP lists 576 classroom hours and 12,000 hours of on-the-job training, backed by transcripts and notarized employer letters. DCP does not accept W-2s, tax returns or unsigned letters as proof.

If you plan to qualify through equivalent experience, gather your documentation early. That review is where many applications stall.

## Costs and fees

These are the published figures as of our research. Fees change, so confirm with DCP and PSI before you pay. DCP's [heating, cooling and sheet metal page](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/License-Services-Division/All-License-Applications/Heating-Cooling-and-Sheet-Metal-Work-Licenses) lists a nonrefundable contractor application fee of $150 and a journeyperson fee of $90, with annual renewal at $150 for contractors and $120 for journeypersons. Those licenses expire August 31.

PSI's [current bulletin](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/49) lists $59 for each exam portion, first attempt or repeat, so a contractor taking Business and Law plus a trade exam pays for two portions. PSI registration fees are not refundable.

For home improvement registration, DCP's [consumer guide](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/dcp/investigations-division/documents/finalhomeimprovementguidepdf.pdf) says $100 of each registration or renewal fee goes to the Home Improvement Guaranty Fund, and registrations expire March 31 each year. Ask DCP for the full current amount.

The NASCLA guide itself is listed at $120 on the [NASCLA bookstore](https://store.nascla.org/collections/connecticut). Our versions are priced in the prep section below.

## Reciprocity and working in Connecticut with an out-of-state license

DCP states on its [heating and cooling license page](https://portal.ct.gov/DCP/License-Services-Division/All-License-Applications/Heating-Cooling-and-Sheet-Metal-Work-Licenses) that Connecticut has no reciprocal agreements with any other jurisdiction. Out-of-state license holders apply through an equivalent out-of-state license path instead, which DCP reviews case by case.

Even if your out-of-state license is accepted as equivalent, expect DCP to tell you which exams you still need. Ask specifically whether Business and Law is required for your license before you schedule anything. Since Connecticut is not a NASCLA commercial exam participant, a NASCLA score from another state is unlikely to help with the trade portion.

## After you pass: license issue, renewal and continuing education

After both portions are passed, send the licensing fee shown on your score report to DCP. Then keep up with renewals. Heating and cooling licenses, for example, renew each year and expire August 31. Home improvement registrations renew by March 31.

Electrical license holders have a continuing education requirement. DCP's [electrician CE page](https://portal.ct.gov/dcp/continuing-education/electrician---continuing-education) lists 4 credit hours per year, completed by June 30, using courses approved by the Connecticut Electrical Board. First-time renewals do not need CE, and you should keep certificates for at least four years in case of an audit. Other trades have their own rules, so check DCP's CE pages for your license.

Keep your 6th Edition guide after the exam. The chapters on contracts, lien law, taxes and employment are useful reference material for running the business day to day.

## Connecticut NASCLA exam and license FAQ

### Does Connecticut accept the NASCLA commercial exam?

Connecticut is not on NASCLA's published list of agencies accepting the commercial general building exam. In Connecticut, NASCLA's role is the Connecticut 6th Edition guide used for the Business and Law exam. Ask DCP if you have an out-of-state license and want to know what testing you still need.

### Do I need a license to be a general contractor in Connecticut?

Connecticut does not issue one general contractor license. Home improvement contractors and new home builders register with DCP, larger commercial builders use the Major Contractor registration, and electrical, plumbing, heating and similar trades need an occupational license.

### How many questions are on the Connecticut Business and Law exam?

PSI's current bulletin lists 50 questions with a two hour time limit. You need 70 percent to pass, and the exam is open book with the Connecticut 6th Edition NASCLA guide.

### Which edition of the NASCLA guide does Connecticut use?

The PSI bulletin lists the Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Connecticut 6th Edition. Older Connecticut editions were used in past years, so check the edition before you buy a used copy.

### Can I bring a highlighted and tabbed book into the PSI exam?

PSI allows permanent tabs only and does not allow sticky notes or other temporary tabs. You may not write in the book during the exam, and the proctor has the final say on whether a reference is accepted.

### Do journeypersons take the Business and Law exam?

Under PSI's bulletin for the electrical trades, journeyperson candidates take only the trade exam. The Business and Law exam is for contractor and dealer candidates.

### Does Connecticut have reciprocity for contractor licenses?

DCP states that Connecticut has no reciprocal agreements with any other jurisdiction. Out-of-state license holders apply through an equivalent out-of-state license path that DCP reviews.

### How long are passed Connecticut exam scores good for?

PSI says each passed portion is valid for two years from the pass date, and contractors must pass both the trade and Business and Law portions. Exam eligibility lasts one year from state approval.
