# Florida NASCLA Exam: How to Get Your Florida Contractor License

By Pass Your NASCLA Exam Prep · 12 min read · 10 official sources cited

## Does Florida accept the NASCLA exam?

Yes, for specific licenses. NASCLA lists the [Florida Construction Industry Licensing Board](https://www.nascla.org/commercial-exam-participating-state-agencies/florida-construction-industry-licensing-board) as accepting its commercial general building exam in place of the Florida trade exam since March 1, 2019. A Florida Business and Finance portion and a Florida Building Code portion are still required.

The board's rule on [certification by endorsement](https://www.flrules.org/gateway/ruleNo.asp?id=61G4-15.004) (61G4-15.004, effective March 10, 2019) spells it out. An applicant for Certified General, Building or Residential Contractor meets the exam requirement by showing a passing NASCLA commercial general building score (the 2009 to 2018 or like version), passing the Florida Business and Finance exam, and passing a proctored Florida Building Code exam given by or through the Department. The board's [endorsement exam list](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/pro/cilb/documents/Endorsement-06.pdf) describes the NASCLA exam from 2009 forward as substantially similar to all Division I trade knowledge exams, which covers general, building and residential contractors and related specialties.

For electrical work, NASCLA's [Florida electrical listing](https://www.nascla.org/electrical-exams-participating-state-agencies/florida-electrical-contractors-licensing-board) says the Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board accepts the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor exam for endorsement on the Certified Specialty Electrical Contractor classification. You must already hold a license in another jurisdiction with a similar scope. If you have never been licensed, the Florida exam is the only option under the statute.

In every case, passing NASCLA covers only the trade exam piece. Experience, credit, background, insurance and the other exams still apply.

## Who issues Florida contractor licenses

Two boards inside the Department of Business and Professional Regulation (DBPR) handle construction. The [Construction Industry Licensing Board](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/construction-industry/) (CILB) licenses general, building, residential, roofing, mechanical, plumbing, pool, underground utility and other contractors. The [Electrical Contractors Licensing Board](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/electrical-contractors/) (ECLB) licenses electrical, alarm system and specialty electrical contractors.

Both boards issue two kinds of credentials. A certified contractor holds a state certificate of competency and can contract anywhere in Florida. A registered contractor has met the competency rules of a particular county or city and can contract only in those jurisdictions. Certified license codes start with C, such as CGC for a certified general contractor, and registered codes start with R.

This split matters for exam planning. The NASCLA commercial route leads to a state certified license. The NASCLA county Business and Law book is used by local licensing offices, typically for local competency cards and registered contractors.

## Florida contractor license types and which exam each needs

Here is how the NASCLA exams line up with Florida credentials, based on the board rules and NASCLA listings cited on this page.

- Certified General, Building or Residential Contractor (Division I) by endorsement: NASCLA Commercial General Building exam, plus Florida Business and Finance, plus a proctored Florida Building Code exam.
- Certified General, Building or Residential Contractor by the standard route: Florida's own trade knowledge exams plus Business and Finance, with no NASCLA exam involved.
- Certified Specialty Electrical Contractor (residential) by endorsement: NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor exam plus the Florida business exam, and only if you hold a similar license in another state.
- First-time electrical applicants with no prior license: the Florida exam only.
- Local competency or registered contractor licenses in participating counties and cities: often a local Business and Law exam given by Prov, using the NASCLA Florida Counties 2nd Edition guide, plus any local trade exam.

## How to get a Florida general contractor license with NASCLA, step by step

This is the endorsement path for a Certified General, Building or Residential Contractor. Confirm each step with CILB before you pay fees.

1. Decide which Division I license fits your work: general, building or residential.
2. Check your experience against section 489.111, such as four years of active experience with at least one year as a foreman, or an allowed mix of college credit and experience.
3. Apply for the NASCLA exam at [ned.nascla.org](https://nascla.org/apply-nascla-exams) and pay the $65 application fee. Wait for approval before scheduling with PSI.
4. Study and pass the NASCLA Commercial General Building exam.
5. Apply through Professional Testing, Inc. for the Florida Business and Finance exam, schedule with Pearson VUE, and pass it.
6. Pass a proctored Florida Building Code exam given by or through DBPR, as the board rule requires.
7. Get a credit report from an acceptable agency, submit fingerprints, and line up workers' compensation coverage or an exemption plus liability and property damage insurance.
8. File the endorsement application with CILB, with your NASCLA score, exam results and experience records.
9. Once approved, qualify your business if you will contract under a company name, and register with any local offices where you will pull permits.

## The Florida NASCLA exam and the electrical exam

The NASCLA commercial general building exam has 115 scored questions and a 330 minute time limit, and it may include some unscored pretest items. It is open book, and you need 70 percent to pass (81 correct answers out of the 115 scored questions). You apply through NASCLA, and after approval you get a candidate ID and a link to schedule with PSI. NASCLA says an approved [application](https://nascla.org/apply-nascla-exams) is good for one year, with up to three attempts in that year. If you run out, you reapply after the year ends.

Because the exam covers a large reference library, the skill being tested is partly how fast you can find things. Project management, jobsite management, concrete, masonry, steel, wood, roofing, safety, accessibility and contracting all appear, each tied to its own book.

The NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor exam, per the current [PSI candidate bulletin](https://test-takers.psiexams.com/api/content/bulletin/6393), has 100 scored items plus 10 unscored ones, a 270 minute limit and a passing score of 70. It is open book, and the bulletin lists the 2020 or 2023 National Electrical Code or NEC Handbook among the allowed references. The same NASCLA application process, one year window and three attempt limit apply.

## The Florida Business and Finance exam

Every NASCLA endorsement candidate for a Division I license still takes Florida Business and Finance. DBPR's [exam summary](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/servop/testing/documents/exam_glance.pdf) lists 120 scored questions over 6.5 hours, given daily on computer. Professional Testing, Inc. handles applications and scoring, and Pearson VUE schedules the [computer exams](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/examination-information/construction-examinations/). All Florida construction exams are open book and multiple choice.

The [content outline](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/servop/testing/documents/bus_fin_eci.pdf) weights accounting functions at 32 percent, administrative duties at 26 percent, government regulations at 15 percent, establishing the business at 11 percent, trade operations at 10 percent and human resources at 6 percent. Accounting alone is nearly a third of the test, so expect real calculations.

DBPR's [testing FAQ](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/servop/testing/documents/cilb_faq.pdf) sets the passing score at 70 percent with no rounding. If you fail Business and Finance, you wait 21 days to retake it, and all parts must be passed within four years of your first attempt. Only the parts you failed need to be retaken.

For the residential electrical endorsement, the ECLB endorsement matrix marks NASCLA as a partial match, meaning you still take the Florida business exam for that board.

## The Florida county Business and Law exam

Many Florida counties and cities run their own contractor licensing and test through Prov. Prov's [Hillsborough County exam descriptions](https://provexam.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/FL-Hillsborough-County-Exam-Descriptions-2025.09.03.pdf) show a Business and Law, 2nd Edition exam with 50 questions in two hours, using the NASCLA Florida guide 2nd edition. Its outline puts 6 questions each on tax laws and labor laws, 5 each on contract management, project management, estimating and bidding and financial management, 4 each on business organization, OSHA safety and risk management, and 3 each on licensing and lien laws. Our product information lists a 75 percent passing score; confirm it on the description Prov sends with your application.

Prov's [Florida bulletin](https://provexam.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Florida-Contractor-Examinations-CIB-2026.06.24.pdf) lists $90 per local test, noting that cost may vary. Highlighting in pen and permanent tabs are permitted, but repositionable tabs and sticky notes are not, and you cannot mark your books during the test. Retakes follow your local jurisdiction's rules.

The book is the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Florida Counties 2nd Edition, which the [NASCLA bookstore](https://store.nascla.org/collections/florida) lists at $120. Ask your local licensing office which exam it requires before you buy, since Prov says it cannot tell you which test your jurisdiction uses.

## Experience, financial, insurance and bond requirements

For Division I contractors, [section 489.111](https://m.flsenate.gov/Statutes/489.111) requires you to be 18 and of good moral character. The experience routes are four years of active experience with at least one year as a foreman, a four year degree in engineering, architecture or building construction plus one year of experience, or a mix of college credit, skilled worker time and at least one year as a foreman.

Under [section 489.115](https://m.flsenate.gov/Statutes/489.115), initial applicants furnish a credit report from a nationally recognized agency and a full set of fingerprints. You also sign an affidavit that you have workers' compensation coverage as Chapter 440 requires, along with public liability and property damage insurance in amounts the board sets by rule. If you claim a workers' compensation exemption, you must get it within 30 days after the license is issued.

Electrical applicants follow [section 489.511](https://m.flsenate.gov/Statutes/489.511). Accepted routes include three years of management experience within the last six, four years as a supervisor or contractor within the last eight, six years of comprehensive training within the last twelve, three years as a licensed electrical engineer, or a mix totaling six years. Endorsement applicants also provide credit information, and business financial records when qualifying a company.

## Reciprocity and other endorsement routes

Section 489.115 lists several ways to be certified by endorsement. Beyond passing a substantially equivalent exam like NASCLA, the law also covers holding a license from a state whose criteria are substantially equivalent, holding a license from a state with a reciprocal agreement, and holding another state's license for at least 10 years and applying for the same or a similar Florida license. That last route requires applying while the other license is active or within 2 years after, and Division I and roofing contractors must complete a 2 hour Florida Building Code course that covers wind mitigation.

CILB publishes a reciprocity and substantially equivalent exam list on its [construction industry page](https://www2.myfloridalicense.com/construction-industry/). Electrical contractors have a parallel endorsement section in 489.511 and an ECLB matrix of accepted exams. If you are licensed elsewhere, compare the routes before you choose; NASCLA may be the simplest path if your state is not on the reciprocity list.

## After you pass: renewal and continuing education

Florida certified and registered contractor licenses renew every two years. Under [section 489.115](https://m.flsenate.gov/Statutes/489.115), CILB licensees complete at least 14 classroom hours each renewal cycle. Part of that must cover workers' compensation, business practices and workplace safety, one hour must cover laws and rules, and some hours must be advanced Florida Building Code courses, as set by board rule.

Electrical contractors renew on the same two year cycle. [Section 489.517](https://m.flsenate.gov/Statutes/489.517) requires at least 11 hours each cycle, with at least 7 technical hours and one hour each on workers' compensation, workplace safety and business practices. Contractors who also do alarm work add 2 hours on false alarm prevention.

Keep your insurance current and update the board when your business or qualifier changes. Local permit offices check your license status before issuing permits.

## Florida NASCLA exam and license FAQ

### Does Florida accept the NASCLA exam for a general contractor license?

Yes. CILB accepts the NASCLA Commercial General Building exam in place of the Florida trade exam for Certified General, Building and Residential Contractor by endorsement. You still must pass Florida Business and Finance and a proctored Florida Building Code exam.

### Do I need an out-of-state license to use NASCLA for a Florida general contractor license?

The board rule for the commercial route asks for the NASCLA score, Business and Finance and a Florida Building Code exam, and does not list another state's license as a condition. The electrical route is different and does require one. Confirm your situation with CILB before you apply.

### Can a first-time electrician use the NASCLA exam in Florida?

No. NASCLA's Florida listing says that applicants who have never been licensed must take the Florida exam. The NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor exam is accepted only for endorsement by someone already licensed elsewhere with a similar scope.

### How many questions are on the Florida Business and Finance exam?

DBPR lists 120 scored questions over 6.5 hours, taken on computer through Pearson VUE. It is open book and the passing score is 70 percent.

### How much experience do I need for a Florida general contractor license?

Section 489.111 calls for four years of active experience with at least one year as a foreman, or a four year construction-related degree plus one year of experience, or certain mixes of college credit and experience. The board reviews your records.

### Is the NASCLA exam open book?

Yes. The commercial exam has 115 scored questions in 330 minutes and you need 70 percent. Bring only the references the current PSI bulletin allows, and follow its rules on tabs and markings.

### What book do Florida counties use for the Business and Law exam?

Prov's Business and Law 2nd Edition exam uses the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Florida Counties 2nd Edition. Ask your county or city licensing office which exam it requires.

### How long do I have to pass all Florida contractor exam parts?

DBPR's testing FAQ says all parts must be passed within four years of your first attempt. For NASCLA, an approved application lasts one year with up to three attempts.

### How often do Florida contractors renew, and how much continuing education is required?

Licenses renew every two years. CILB licensees need at least 14 hours per cycle and electrical contractors need at least 11, with set topics for each.
