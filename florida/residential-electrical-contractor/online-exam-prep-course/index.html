Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Examination Program offers three electrical trade examinations: Electrical Contractor or Master Electrician, Journeyman Electrician, and Residential Electrical Contractor. The Residential Electrical Contractor examination is specifically focused on the knowledge and skills associated with residential electrical contracting.

The NASCLA electrical examinations are standardized trade examinations designed for participating licensing agencies. A passing examination can be used with participating jurisdictions according to each agency's own licensing and endorsement rules. Passing NASCLA does not create a national license and does not automatically authorize a contractor to work in Florida.

For Florida, NASCLA identifies the Residential Electrical Contractor examination as an accepted examination for endorsement on the Florida Certified Specialty Electrical Contractor classification. Florida's Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board remains responsible for reviewing the applicant's qualifications and determining whether the individual is eligible for certification.

Residential electrical exam preparation should include a broad range of technical knowledge. Candidates should be ready to work with residential branch circuits, services, feeders, grounding and bonding, conductor sizing, overcurrent protection, wiring methods, boxes, raceways, equipment, appliances, motors, lighting, safety requirements, fire alarm systems, and residential construction provisions.

Electrical theory is also an important part of preparation. Candidates should understand voltage, current, resistance, power, series and parallel circuits, alternating-current concepts, electrical loads, conductor calculations, and practical troubleshooting. Rather than studying each topic in isolation, candidates should practice connecting electrical theory with National Electrical Code applications.

NASCLA examination applications are submitted through the NASCLA National Examination Database. NASCLA currently lists a $65 application fee. Applications are typically processed within seven business days, and an approved application is valid for one year from the approval date.

Open Book Test

The NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination is an open book test. NASCLA identifies all three examinations in its Accredited Electrical Examination Program as open-book electrical trade examinations.

Open-book testing means effective preparation should include regular reference navigation. Candidates should become comfortable identifying the subject of a question, selecting the appropriate reference, locating the relevant rule, table, formula, or technical requirement, and applying the information correctly.

The National Electrical Code should receive significant attention during preparation. Learn how the NEC is organized and practice using the index, Article 100 definitions, chapters, individual articles, tables, exceptions, and cross-references. Residential electrical questions may require candidates to move between general electrical requirements and rules applying specifically to dwelling units, equipment, wiring methods, services, feeders, branch circuits, and special systems.

Safety references should also be used actively during study. OSHA 29 CFR Part 1926 addresses construction-industry safety requirements, while NFPA 70E focuses on electrical safety in the workplace. Candidates should learn which reference is most appropriate for different types of safety questions.

Open-book preparation should not depend on looking up every answer from scratch. The strongest study approach combines technical understanding with efficient navigation. Practice recognizing the subject first, determining where it is addressed, locating the supporting material, and then applying it to the problem.

Licensing Steps

Florida's acceptance of the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination applies to an endorsement pathway rather than serving as the standard examination route for every new Florida applicant.

  1. Confirm that the Florida endorsement pathway applies to you. Applicants using the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination for Florida must hold a license from another jurisdiction that is similar in scope to the Florida classification being requested.
  2. Review Florida certification qualifications. Florida requires applicants for electrical contractor certification to satisfy statutory experience, character, and licensing requirements.
  3. Apply for the NASCLA examination. Submit the NASCLA examination application through the National Examination Database and complete the applicable application requirements.
  4. Prepare for the Residential Electrical Contractor examination. Study the National Electrical Code, residential code provisions, OSHA safety, electrical workplace safety, theory, calculations, fire alarm systems, product safety markings, and business and project management subjects.
  5. Take and pass the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination. Follow all current examination rules concerning approved reference materials and testing procedures.
  6. Request the appropriate NASCLA examination documentation. NASCLA provides electronic transcript services for participating licensing agencies.
  7. Complete the Florida endorsement application. Submit the application and documentation required by the Florida Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board.
  8. Document your out-of-state license. The license must be from another jurisdiction and similar in scope to the Florida certification being pursued.
  9. Complete Florida's remaining certification requirements. The Board determines whether all applicable statutory and regulatory requirements have been satisfied before issuing certification.

State Requirements

Florida electrical contractor certification is regulated by the Electrical Contractors' Licensing Board within the Department of Business and Professional Regulation. Florida law provides both examination and endorsement pathways for electrical and specialty electrical contractor certification.

A person must be at least 18 years old to take the Florida electrical contractor certification examination. An applicant seeking certification must also satisfy Florida's good-moral-character requirement and meet one of the experience or education pathways established under Florida law.

One pathway requires at least three years of proven management experience in the trade during the six years immediately preceding the application. Education may substitute for part of this requirement, but no more than one-half of the required experience may be satisfied through educational equivalency.

Another pathway allows qualification based on at least four years of experience as a supervisor or contractor in the trade during the eight years immediately preceding the application. Florida also recognizes qualifying supervisory electrical or alarm-system experience obtained through the United States Armed Forces.

Florida also provides a pathway based on at least six years of comprehensive training, technical education, or supervisory experience associated with an electrical or alarm-system contracting business during the 12 years immediately preceding the application. Qualifying technical electrical experience, education, or training obtained through the United States Armed Forces or a governmental entity may also be considered under this pathway.

A qualifying professional engineer may use another statutory pathway when the individual has been licensed for at least three years and is qualified by education, training, or experience to practice electrical engineering. Florida also permits certain combinations of qualifying experience, education, and training.

The Florida Residential Electrical Contractor specialty scope includes the installation, repair, alteration, addition to, replacement of, or design of electrical wiring, fixtures, appliances, apparatus, raceways, conduit, or related electrical components in one-, two-, three-, or four-family residences not exceeding two stories in height, together with accessory-use structures associated with those residences. The electrical service within this specialty scope is limited to single-phase, 400-ampere single service.

For NASCLA specifically, Florida currently accepts the Residential Electrical Contractor examination for endorsement. NASCLA states that the applicant must hold a license from another jurisdiction that is similar in scope to the Florida classification being requested.

An applicant who has never previously held a license cannot use the NASCLA examination as a substitute for Florida's initial examination pathway. For an initial applicant with no prior qualifying license, Florida requires the applicable Florida examination.

Test Information and Study Materials

Residential electrical exam preparation is most effective when candidates combine code study, electrical theory, calculations, safety, and repeated reference navigation. The goal is to understand both why an electrical requirement applies and where to find the supporting rule.

Begin with the 2023 National Electrical Code. Concentrate on dwelling-unit branch circuits, required receptacle and lighting outlets, services, feeders, conductor sizing, load calculations, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, wiring methods, boxes, raceways, equipment, appliances, motors, and special systems.

Electrical theory should be studied alongside NEC requirements. Review Ohm's law, power formulas, series and parallel circuits, conductor relationships, voltage drop concepts, alternating-current fundamentals, and practical calculations. Understanding theory helps candidates recognize what a calculation question is asking before selecting a formula.

The International Residential Code adds broader residential construction context. Candidates should understand how electrical installations interact with residential building conditions and how code requirements affect work in houses, townhouses, and related structures.

Safety preparation should include construction-jobsite requirements from OSHA 29 CFR Part 1926 and electrical safe-work practices from NFPA 70E. ANSI Z535.4 provides additional preparation involving product safety signs and hazard communication.

NFPA 72 supports review of residential fire alarm and signaling concepts. Ugly's Electrical References can reinforce frequently used formulas and field calculations, while Understanding Electrical Theory for NEC Applications can help connect electrical principles directly to code-based problem solving.

Business and project management should not be neglected. The NASCLA Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management supports study of contractor operations, estimating, contracts, project administration, safety management, financial topics, and other business responsibilities.

A strong preparation routine combines topic review with practice-oriented questions. After answering a question, locate the supporting information in the applicable reference even when the answer seems familiar. This reinforces technical understanding and improves open-book navigation.
