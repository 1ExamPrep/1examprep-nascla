It is part of the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program, which provides standardized trade examinations accepted by participating licensing agencies.

  • Classification: Electrical Contractors / Master Electricians / Unlimited Electricians
  • Scored Questions: 100
  • Minimum Passing Score: 70

State Requirements

Electrical licensing in Idaho is regulated by the Idaho Electrical Board through the Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses. Idaho licenses Electrical Contractors, Master Electricians, Journeyman Electricians, and additional electrical specialties under its electrical licensing laws and rules.

Idaho is a participating jurisdiction in the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program. The state accepts the NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination and the NASCLA Journeyman Electrician examination.

The individual Master Electrician license and the Electrical Contractor license serve different purposes. A Master Electrician is an individual professional credential, while an Electrical Contractor license authorizes the contracting business to perform electrical contracting work within the scope allowed by the license and supervising electrician.

For the Idaho Master Electrician experience pathway, an applicant must have held an Electrical Journeyman license for at least four years. Idaho also provides a reciprocity pathway when the applicant satisfies the applicable license-verification conditions, including proof of qualifying journeyman licensure and an active master license.

Idaho electrical contractor applicants must pass an examination designated by the Electrical Board and submit the applicable application. Each electrical contractor must designate one supervising electrician who is responsible for the activities performed under the contractor license.

The supervising electrician must hold an Idaho electrical credential appropriate for the scope of work being performed. A master electrician, journeyman electrician, residential electrician, or limited electrical installer may serve as a supervising electrician when the contractor's work falls within that person's licensed scope of practice.

Idaho rules also specify that the contractor's allowable scope of electrical work follows the scope of its licensed supervising employee. This makes the supervising electrician an important part of the contractor licensing structure and distinguishes the business license from the individual electrical credential.

Passing the NASCLA examination does not automatically create an Idaho Master Electrician or Electrical Contractor license. Candidates must still apply through the Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses and satisfy the experience, supervision, application, and other requirements associated with the credential they are pursuing.

Idaho electrical examinations are administered through PSI. After achieving the required passing result, applicants proceed with the appropriate Idaho licensing application through the Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses.

Test Information and Study Materials

Highlighted and tabbed references are most useful when candidates work with them throughout their preparation. Use the tabs during regular Code review, electrical calculations, safety research, and practice questions so the location of major subjects becomes familiar.

Start with the 2023 National Electrical Code and practice navigating services, feeders, branch circuits, conductor sizing, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, wiring methods, motors, transformers, equipment, and special installations. Learn to use both the index and the Code's internal cross-references.

Electrical theory should be reinforced with active problem solving. Practice voltage, current, resistance, power, motors, transformers, and related calculations. Develop a consistent method for determining what the question asks, selecting the correct formula, completing the calculation, and checking whether a Code rule changes the result.

Use OSHA Part 1926 and NFPA 70E for focused safety review. Learn which reference applies to construction safety and which addresses workplace electrical safety. This distinction can make reference searches more efficient.

Project-management and contractor-business references should also remain part of the study schedule. Electrical contracting involves more than installation knowledge, and candidates should become familiar with planning, coordination, risk, communication, estimating concepts, and professional project responsibilities.

Finally, practice under timed conditions. Work through sample questions using the actual highlighted and tabbed references. Track how quickly you identify the subject, select the correct book, reach the relevant section, and confirm the answer. This approach helps combine technical understanding with practical open-book navigation.
