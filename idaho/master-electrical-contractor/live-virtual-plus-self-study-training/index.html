Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Trade Examination for Electrical Contractors is part of the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program. NASCLA offers standardized electrical examinations for Electrical Contractors, Journeyman Electricians, and Residential Electrical Contractors. Participating jurisdictions may accept the applicable examination as part of their own licensing process.

The Electrical Contractor examination is designed for advanced electrical professionals and covers a broad range of trade knowledge. Preparation should include electrical theory, Code application, personal and jobsite safety, grounding and bonding, wiring methods, circuit protection, electrical equipment, project-management concepts, electrical calculations, special systems, and energy-related material.

Because the examination is built around multiple technical references, candidates should become comfortable recognizing which source is most likely to contain the information needed. A wiring-method question may require the National Electrical Code, while a construction-safety question may involve OSHA Part 1926. Workplace electrical-safety questions may direct candidates to NFPA 70E, while electrical-theory questions may require formulas, calculations, or supporting technical references.

Idaho's current Master Electrician licensing application asks initial applicants whether they have taken and passed the applicable NASCLA examination through PSI. Applicants who took the examination outside Idaho are instructed to include a copy of their test scores with the application.

The examination is only one component of Idaho licensure. A passing examination result does not automatically issue a Master Electrician or Electrical Contractor license. Applicants must complete the Idaho application process and satisfy the licensing qualifications that apply to the credential they are pursuing.

Open Book Test

The NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program uses open-book electrical trade examinations. Candidates may use authorized references according to the applicable NASCLA and testing-provider requirements.

Open-book testing does not remove the need to understand electrical concepts. Technical references contain extensive information, and attempting to search every question from the beginning can consume valuable time. Candidates should develop enough subject knowledge to identify the likely topic before opening a book.

The 2023 National Electrical Code should be a major part of preparation. Candidates should practice using the NEC index, definitions, article structure, tables, exceptions, and cross-references. Study areas can include branch circuits, feeders, services, conductor sizing, ampacity, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, wiring methods, motors, transformers, equipment, special occupancies, and specialty electrical systems.

Electrical theory should be studied through active problem solving. Practice voltage, current, resistance, power, circuit relationships, motors, transformers, and other fundamental calculations. A useful process is to identify the information given, determine what must be solved, choose the appropriate formula, complete the calculation, and then determine whether an NEC requirement affects the final answer.

Safety preparation should include OSHA Part 1926 and NFPA 70E. OSHA Part 1926 addresses federal construction-safety requirements, while NFPA 70E focuses on electrical safety in the workplace. Learning the purpose and organization of each reference can make open-book research more efficient.

Live virtual training can help explain how these different subjects and books connect, while self-study gives candidates the opportunity to revisit difficult concepts, repeat calculations, practice Code lookups, and spend additional time on technical areas that need greater attention.

Licensing Steps

  1. Determine the Idaho electrical credential you need. Idaho licenses Master Electricians, Journeyman Electricians, Electrical Contractors, and several specialty electrical classifications.
  2. Review the Master Electrician qualification requirements. Idaho requires Master Electrician applicants using the experience pathway to have at least four years of experience as a licensed journeyman electrician.
  3. Complete the applicable examination requirement. Idaho's Master Electrician application recognizes the applicable NASCLA examination through PSI.
  4. Prepare with live and self-study training. Review electrical theory, Code application, calculations, safety, project management, and reference navigation through structured instruction and independent study.
  5. Take and pass the applicable examination. Applicants must obtain the required passing examination result before completing the applicable Idaho licensing process.
  6. Submit the Master Electrician application when applicable. Initial applicants provide the completed application, passing examination information, and other required documentation to the Idaho Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses.
  7. Apply separately for an Electrical Contractor license when needed. Idaho treats the contractor license and individual electrician license as separate credentials.
  8. Designate a supervising electrician for contractor licensing. An Idaho electrical contractor must operate under an appropriately licensed supervising electrician.
  9. Match the contractor scope to the supervising license. Idaho rules tie the contractor's allowable scope of work to the scope of its licensed supervising employee.
  10. Maintain the applicable license. Licensed electricians and contractors must comply with Idaho's renewal and practice requirements.

State Requirements

Electrical licensing in Idaho is administered by the Idaho Electrical Board through the Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses. Idaho separately licenses Master Electricians, Journeyman Electricians, Electrical Contractors, limited electrical contractors, and other electrical classifications.

A Master Electrician license is an individual credential. Idaho's licensing rules establish that an applicant for a Master Electrician license must have at least four years of experience as a licensed journeyman electrician before qualifying for the master-level examination pathway.

Idaho's current Master Electrician application recognizes the NASCLA examination. The application asks whether the applicant has taken and passed the applicable NASCLA examination through PSI and requires a passing examination result before the license application is completed. Applicants who tested outside Idaho must provide a copy of their test scores.

An Idaho Electrical Contractor license is separate from the Master Electrician credential. Contractor applicants must complete the contractor licensing requirements established by the Idaho Electrical Board and must operate with an appropriately licensed supervising electrician.

Idaho's current electrical rules state that a contractor's allowable scope of work is the same as the scope of its licensed employee. The supervising electrician must be available during working hours and is responsible for supervising electrical installations performed by the contractor.

The supervising electrician may be a Master Electrician, Journeyman Electrician, Residential Electrician, or Limited Electrical Installer when the contractor's work falls within that individual's authorized scope. This licensing structure makes it important to distinguish the business contractor license from the individual electrical license used to supervise the work.

An individual electrical contractor may serve as his or her own supervising electrician when the individual holds the appropriate active electrical credential for the work being performed. For businesses, the supervising electrician provides the licensed technical supervision required for electrical installations.

Passing a NASCLA examination does not automatically issue an Idaho license. The examination can satisfy an applicable testing requirement, but the applicant must still submit the required application and meet the experience, supervision, and licensing requirements associated with the requested credential.

Test Information and Study Materials

A productive study plan should combine structured technical instruction with repeated independent practice. Live virtual training helps candidates organize a broad examination scope into focused subjects, while self-study gives candidates more time to reinforce difficult concepts and develop stronger reference-navigation habits.

Start with the 2023 National Electrical Code and use it as a working reference. Practice using the index, following cross-references, locating tables, reviewing definitions, and moving between related articles. The goal is to become comfortable finding information rather than relying entirely on memorized article numbers.

Electrical calculations should also be practiced consistently. Work through voltage, current, resistance, power, circuit relationships, motors, transformers, and other electrical problems. Focus on understanding the reasoning behind each calculation and recognizing when an NEC requirement must be applied.

Safety study should include direct use of OSHA Part 1926 and NFPA 70E. Learn which subjects belong in each reference and how each book is organized. This can help candidates avoid searching through the wrong resource when working through safety questions.

Project-management and contractor-business material should remain part of preparation. Electrical contractors operate within projects that require planning, communication, coordination, documentation, scheduling, risk management, and professional organization. Energy-efficiency references add another technical area candidates should learn to navigate.

Timed reference exercises can help candidates connect technical knowledge with open-book preparation. Select a practice question, identify the subject, choose the most likely reference, locate the supporting information, and measure the time required. Repeated practice can show which subjects and books need additional attention.

Live instruction can establish the study structure, while self-study provides the repetition that helps make that structure useful. Candidates can revisit concepts covered in class, repeat difficult calculations, and spend additional time on Code navigation or safety material between scheduled sessions.
