The examination is based on an occupational analysis developed with electrical industry subject matter experts and is intended to evaluate the knowledge required to perform electrical work safely and competently.

Electrical Theory also represents a significant portion of the examination. Candidates should be prepared to work with fundamental electrical relationships, formulas, circuits, and calculations. Successful preparation should include solving problems instead of relying solely on reading or memorization.

Safety, project design and management, special occupancies and equipment, and communication systems complete the examination scope. A balanced study program should address all of these areas rather than focusing exclusively on the NEC subjects encountered most frequently in everyday work.

Open Book Test

The NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program consists of open-book electrical trade examinations, including the Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination. Candidates are responsible for bringing approved reference materials into the examination center and following the current rules governing those references.

Reference materials used in NASCLA testing may be subject to specific preparation requirements. NASCLA examination guidance permits approved references to be highlighted, underlined, and permanently indexed while otherwise remaining unmarked. Additional loose or attached papers are not permitted, and temporary indexing materials such as Post-it notes are not allowed.

An open-book examination still requires extensive preparation. The references contain a large amount of information, and searching every question from the beginning of a book can consume valuable testing time. Candidates should develop familiarity with the organization of each major reference before taking the examination.

National Electrical Code navigation should receive particular attention. Become comfortable locating definitions, tables, conductor requirements, branch-circuit provisions, feeder and service requirements, grounding and bonding provisions, wiring methods, overcurrent protection rules, motor requirements, transformer provisions, and requirements for specialized installations.

Practice with supporting references as well. Safety questions may involve OSHA construction regulations or NFPA 70E. Electrical theory may require textbook knowledge or calculation references. Project-management questions can draw on project and contractor-management material, while energy-related concepts may involve the applicable energy-efficiency standard.

A practical study method is to answer practice questions with the books open while timing your searches. Identify the subject first, select the reference most likely to contain the answer, locate the relevant material, and confirm how it applies. Repetition can improve both recognition and navigation speed.

State Requirements

Electrical licensing in Idaho is administered through the Idaho Electrical Board within the Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses. Idaho maintains separate licenses for master electricians, electrical contractors, journeyman electricians, specialty electrical contractors, and other regulated electrical classifications.

An applicant pursuing an Idaho master electrician license through the experience pathway must have held an electrical journeyman license for at least four years. Idaho also provides a reciprocity pathway for qualifying applicants who hold an active master license from another jurisdiction and satisfy the state's verification requirements.

The Idaho Electrical Board participates in the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program. NASCLA currently lists Idaho as accepting both the Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination and the NASCLA Journeyman Electrician examination.

An Idaho electrical contractor license has additional requirements beyond an individual's master electrician qualification. Idaho requires an electrical contractor to have at least one full-time employee who holds a valid Idaho master electrician license. That individual serves as the supervising electrician and must be available during working hours to carry out the supervision responsibilities required under Idaho law.

An individual electrical contractor may serve as the contractor's own supervising master electrician when that individual holds the required valid master electrician license. Business entities must satisfy the applicable contractor application and supervisory requirements established by the Idaho Electrical Board.

Idaho requires an electrical contractor license before a person or business acts or attempts to act as an electrical contractor within the scope regulated by the state. Electrical contracting includes covered installation, maintenance, and repair activities.

Passing a NASCLA examination does not automatically create an Idaho license. NASCLA provides an accepted examination credential, while the Idaho Electrical Board retains responsibility for experience qualifications, contractor supervision requirements, applications, and issuance of Idaho licenses.

After satisfying the applicable examination requirement, candidates must submit their licensing application through the Idaho Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses. Applicants should make sure the application corresponds to the specific credential they intend to hold, because master electrician and electrical contractor licenses serve different regulatory purposes.

Test Information and Study Materials

A strong NASCLA preparation plan should combine Code review, electrical theory, calculations, safety, and reference navigation. Simply reading the reference books from beginning to end may not provide the active practice needed for an open-book professional examination.

Begin with the highest-volume NEC subjects. General Code Requirements, Wiring and Protection, Wiring Methods and Materials, and Equipment for General Use account for a large portion of the published examination blueprint. Review services, feeders, branch circuits, conductor sizing, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, cables, raceways, boxes, motors, transformers, and common equipment requirements.

When reviewing Code subjects, practice finding the actual controlling provision. A question may involve multiple related sections, exceptions, tables, or definitions. Learning how those provisions work together can make it easier to analyze application-based questions.

Electrical theory should be practiced through calculations. Start by identifying the values provided in a problem, determine what must be solved, select the appropriate formula, maintain consistent units, and complete the calculation carefully. Then determine whether an NEC provision affects the final selection or result.

Safety preparation should include familiarity with both construction safety and electrical workplace safety. OSHA Part 1926 and NFPA 70E serve different purposes, so candidates should learn which reference applies to the safety concept being tested.

Project design and management should not be neglected. Electrical contractors need to understand work planning, coordination, project responsibilities, and management concepts in addition to installation requirements. Include this material throughout the study schedule rather than leaving it until the final days of preparation.

Timed practice is particularly useful for an open-book exam. Monitor how quickly you recognize the subject, choose the correct reference, locate the information, and solve the question. If certain topics repeatedly require long searches, spend additional time learning where those subjects are located in the applicable reference.
