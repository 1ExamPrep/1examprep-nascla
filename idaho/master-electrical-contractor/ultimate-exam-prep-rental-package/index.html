Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Trade Examination for Electrical Contractors is part of the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program. The program offers an open-book examination for Electrical Contractors, Master Electricians, and Unlimited Electricians and is recognized by participating state licensing agencies.

The Idaho Electrical Board, operating through the Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses, is a participating agency in the NASCLA electrical program. Idaho accepts the NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination and the NASCLA Journeyman Electrician examination.

Idaho's Master Electrician application specifically recognizes the applicable NASCLA examination. Applicants must pass the required examination before submitting the license application. If a NASCLA examination was taken outside Idaho, the application requires the applicant to include the applicable test scores with the submission.

The NASCLA Electrical Contractor examination is designed around advanced electrical trade knowledge. Preparation should include National Electrical Code requirements, electrical theory, calculations, safety, wiring methods, circuit protection, grounding and bonding, electrical equipment, special systems, project management, and related technical subjects.

A complete preparation plan should use the full reference set. The National Electrical Code is a central resource, but other books address electrical theory, construction safety, workplace electrical safety, energy efficiency, project management, and professional contractor responsibilities.

Candidates should also distinguish between Idaho's individual Master Electrician license and business-related contractor requirements. The NASCLA examination may satisfy an examination component, but Idaho's licensing authority still determines whether the applicant meets the requirements for the requested credential.

Open Book Test

The NASCLA Electrical Contractor examination is an open-book test. Approved reference materials may be used according to the current NASCLA and testing-provider requirements.

Open-book testing requires both technical understanding and efficient reference use. Candidates should practice recognizing the subject of each problem before selecting a book. This makes it easier to determine whether a question belongs in the NEC, a safety reference, an electrical theory text, a project-management resource, or another supporting book.

The 2023 National Electrical Code should receive extensive navigation practice. Candidates should become comfortable using definitions, the index, articles, tables, exceptions, cross-references, and the organizational structure of the Code. Important study areas include branch circuits, feeders, services, conductor sizing, ampacity, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, wiring methods, motors, transformers, equipment, special occupancies, and specialized electrical systems.

Electrical theory should be reinforced through active calculations. Practice voltage, current, resistance, power, circuit relationships, motors, transformers, and other technical concepts. A consistent calculation process can help candidates identify known values, determine what must be solved, select the correct formula, and then determine whether an NEC requirement affects the result.

Safety study should include OSHA Part 1926 and NFPA 70E. OSHA Part 1926 addresses construction workplace safety, while NFPA 70E focuses on electrical safety in the workplace. Candidates should become familiar with the purpose and layout of each reference.

The highlighted and tabbed format supports repeated navigation practice throughout the one-year course-access period. Candidates can use the books while completing exercises and practice questions so that major sections and reference categories become increasingly familiar before examination day.

State Requirements

Electrical licensing in Idaho is administered through the Idaho Electrical Board within the Division of Occupational and Professional Licenses. The Board regulates individual electrical licenses and contractor-related requirements within the state.

Idaho participates in the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program and accepts the NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination. The state also accepts the NASCLA Journeyman Electrician examination.

For Master Electrician applicants, Idaho's application materials require the applicable NASCLA examination to be passed before the license application is submitted. Applicants who took the examination outside Idaho must include a copy of their examination scores.

The NASCLA examination is not a national license. It is an examination credential that can satisfy an accepted trade-examination requirement in participating jurisdictions. Idaho applicants must still complete the state licensing process and satisfy all additional requirements that apply.

Idaho also regulates contractor businesses separately from individual electrician credentials. Contractor registration requirements can include business ownership information, workers' compensation documentation or an applicable exemption statement, and general liability insurance.

Idaho contractor registration materials require general liability insurance of at least $300,000 single limit, including products and continued operations coverage. Workers' compensation insurance documentation is also required when applicable.

Contractor applicants must provide identifying and ownership information for the business and specify the type of construction activity undertaken. This business registration structure is distinct from the individual electrical credential held by a Master Electrician or Journeyman Electrician.

Candidates preparing for electrical contracting should therefore distinguish between the individual technical credential and the requirements applying to the contracting business. An examination result may satisfy one component, while business registration, insurance, supervision, and other licensing requirements remain separate.

The Idaho Electrical Board's rules and statutes govern electrical contractors and journeymen. Candidates should plan their licensing process around the credential and business structure they intend to use rather than treating the NASCLA examination as the final licensing step.
