Maryland MHIC Exam: How to Get Your Home Improvement License

By Pass Your NASCLA Exam Prep · 11 min read · 8 official sources cited

If you want to sell or perform home improvement work in Maryland, you need a license from the Maryland Home Improvement Commission, usually called MHIC. Before you can apply, you have to pass an open book exam given by PSI. Contractors take a 55 question exam and salespeople take a 35 question exam.

Both exams are built around books published by NASCLA, the National Association of State Contractors Licensing Agencies. Contractors test from the Maryland Home Improvement Commission edition of the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, now in its 7th Edition. Salespeople test from the NASCLA Home Improvement Salesperson Reference Guide, Maryland, 6th Edition. This page explains how the MHIC license works, what each exam covers, what it costs, and how to prepare with the right book. Orders from Pass Your NASCLA Exam Prep are fulfilled by 1 Exam Prep.

Does Maryland Accept the NASCLA Exam?

This is the first thing to get straight. Maryland does not appear on NASCLA's list of agencies that accept the NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors, so passing that 115 question commercial exam does not get you an MHIC license. Check the current list on NASCLA's participating agencies page if you hold the commercial exam from another state.

What Maryland does use is NASCLA's publishing. The MHIC writes its own contractor and salesperson exams, and PSI delivers them, but the designated references are NASCLA books written for Maryland. That is why people search for a Maryland NASCLA exam: the test is a state exam, and the book you bring into the room is a NASCLA guide.

The MHIC also states that it has no reciprocity with other states. A contractor licensed elsewhere still has to pass the Maryland exam before getting an MHIC license. So whatever your background, plan on taking the MHIC exam.

Who Issues the Maryland Home Improvement License

The Maryland Home Improvement Commission sits within the Maryland Department of Labor. It licenses contractors and salespersons who solicit or do home improvement work, keeps a Guaranty Fund for homeowners, and handles complaints and unlicensed activity.

Home improvement under the MHIC generally means work on existing homes and on apartment buildings with three or fewer units: remodeling, repair, replacement and similar improvements. The Commission says a licensed MHIC contractor does not need a separate business construction license. Building brand new homes is different. New home builders register with the Attorney General's Home Builder Registration Unit, because the MHIC license does not cover new home construction.

Out of state contractors are not exempt. If you solicit or perform home improvement work in Maryland, you need the MHIC license even without a Maryland office, and you also need to register with the State Department of Assessments and Taxation. Each licensed business needs at least one individual who holds the MHIC license and meets the requirements.

MHIC Contractor License and Salesperson License Types

There are two main paths, and each has its own exam and its own reference book.

Remember that a salesperson license is tied to contractors. You need a written notice signed by you and a licensed contractor that shows the working relationship. You can represent no more than two licensed contractors, and only one of them when you negotiate a contract with the same homeowner.

  • MHIC contractor license: for the business owner or individual who contracts for home improvement work. Exam: the 55 question MHIC contractor exam. Reference: NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Maryland Home Improvement Commission, 7th Edition.
  • MHIC salesperson license: for people who sell home improvements on behalf of a licensed contractor. Exam: the 35 question MHIC salesperson exam. Reference: NASCLA Home Improvement Salesperson Reference Guide, Maryland, 6th Edition.
  • Both exams also use Maryland's Door-to-Door Sales Act (Title 14, Subtitle 3), which PSI provides at the test site.

How to Get a Maryland Home Improvement License, Step by Step

The order matters in Maryland. You pass the exam first, then you apply to the Commission. Here is the path for a contractor. Salespeople follow the same exam steps and then submit the salesperson application with the signed contractor notice.

  1. Confirm your work is home improvement under the MHIC and not new home building or marine work, which other agencies regulate.
  2. Check that you have two years of home improvement or construction experience, related education, or a mix, and that you can document it.
  3. Download the current MHIC Candidate Information Bulletin from PSI and read the reference rules.
  4. Get the correct NASCLA book: the 7th Edition MHIC guide for contractors or the 6th Edition salesperson guide for salespeople. Add permanent tabs if you want them.
  5. Register and pay with PSI online. PSI stopped taking applications by mail, fax and email in 2025.
  6. Schedule, study, and pass the exam with at least 70 percent.
  7. Within two years of passing, submit your MHIC application with proof of experience, financial solvency, $50,000 liability insurance, business registration documents and the fees.
  8. Wait for the Commission's review. It says processing usually takes two to three weeks when the application is complete.

The Maryland MHIC Exam: Format, Rules and Scoring

Both MHIC exams are computer based and open book, delivered by PSI. According to the PSI bulletin updated October 10, 2025, the contractor exam has 55 questions, a 150 minute time limit and a passing score of 39 correct. The salesperson exam has 35 questions, 90 minutes and a passing score of 25 correct. Both work out to 70 percent.

The contractor exam breaks down this way: Home Improvement Law 41 questions, Door-to-Door Sales Act 4, Labor Laws 3, Safety Regulations 4, and Estimating 3. The salesperson exam is Home Improvement Law 25 and Door-to-Door Sales Act 10. Home improvement law carries most of the score on both, so your book's licensing and law chapters deserve the most attention.

PSI allows only the listed references. You may highlight, underline and index your book, but it must otherwise be free of writing and loose or attached papers. Permanent tabs such as Avery Swift Tabs or Redi-Tag permanent index tabs are acceptable. Post-it flags are not, and tabs that peel off without tearing the page have to come off before you test. The proctor makes the final call.

If you fail, PSI requires a 30 day wait before your first retake and 60 days before each later one, with a new fee each time. Your passing result is good for two years. Read the current PSI Candidate Information Bulletin before test day since rules can change.

The Maryland Business and Law Reference: NASCLA 7th and 6th Editions

Maryland does not have a separate business and law exam on top of a trade exam. The MHIC contractor exam is itself a business and law exam, and its book is the Maryland Home Improvement Commission edition of NASCLA's Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management. The current PSI bulletin lists the 7th Edition, and NASCLA's Maryland store page sells the 7th Edition along with a Spanish language version.

The 7th Edition is organized in four parts. The first covers Maryland licensing, estimating, employees, lien law, safety and environmental topics. The second walks through the home improvement license application, fees and sample forms. The third covers business structure, management, insurance and marketing, and the fourth covers planning, scheduling, project management, subcontractors, financial administration and taxes.

Salesperson candidates use the NASCLA Home Improvement Salesperson Reference Guide, Maryland, 6th Edition. It is a shorter book focused on what a salesperson needs: home improvement law and door to door sales rules. If you are taking the salesperson exam, do not buy the contractor guide by mistake, and the reverse is also true.

Experience, Financial Solvency and Insurance Requirements

For a contractor license, the MHIC asks for two years of experience in home improvement or construction, related education, or both, and you must prove it.

You also have to show financial solvency based on the size and scope of your business. The Commission looks at total assets, liabilities, a full credit report and net worth. Its FAQ says solvency is judged on your personal assets rather than the business's. If you fall short of the guidelines, you can buy a surety bond or bring in an indemnitor.

Insurance is fixed at a published figure: you need current general liability coverage of $50,000, both when you first apply and at each renewal. It must stay in force, and the Commission must get notice at least 10 days before any cancellation.

Corporations submit articles of incorporation and a certificate of good standing from the State Department of Assessments and Taxation. If you use a trade name, check that it is available with the Commission, register it, and include the registration certificate. Applicants with certain criminal convictions must also send court records.

These requirements support the Home Improvement Guaranty Fund, which can pay homeowners up to $20,000 for unworkmanlike, inadequate or incomplete work by a licensed contractor.

MHIC License Costs and Fees

These are the published figures on the MHIC Forms and Fees page at the time of writing. Fees change, so confirm with the Commission before you pay. The PSI exam fee is $63 per attempt.

Budget for more than the Commission's fees. You also pay for the exam, your reference book, liability insurance, and any surety bond if you need one for financial solvency. If you later put a contractor license on inactive status, the fee page lists a $126.75 change of status fee for contractors and $42.25 for salespersons.

  • Original contractor license (two years): $281.25 application fee, $281.25 for each additional location, a $100 Guaranty Fund assessment, and a $22.50 processing fee.
  • Original salesperson license (two years): $112.50 application fee and a $22.50 processing fee.
  • Contractor renewal: $281.25, plus $281.25 per additional location and a $175 Guaranty Fund assessment.
  • Salesperson renewal: $112.50.

After You Pass the MHIC Exam

Your passing score lasts two years. If you do not apply for the license in that window, you have to take the whole exam again, so do not let it sit. Gather your experience records, insurance certificate and business documents while you study so the application is ready when your score comes in.

Once the Commission approves your application, it mails a paper license, which it says goes out within about 10 days of approval. The license period is two years from the date the original license is issued. To renew, you pay the renewal and Guaranty Fund fees and show current proof of $50,000 liability insurance.

Keep your book. The 7th Edition guide also works as a desk reference for contracts, estimating, insurance and running the business once you are licensed. You can check any contractor's status with the MHIC license search on the Maryland Department of Labor site.

Maryland NASCLA exam and license FAQ

Does Maryland accept the NASCLA commercial exam for the MHIC license?

No. Maryland is not on NASCLA's list of agencies accepting the commercial general building exam, and the MHIC has no reciprocity with other states. You need to pass the MHIC contractor or salesperson exam through PSI. The NASCLA connection is the reference book, which NASCLA publishes for Maryland.

How many questions are on the Maryland MHIC exam?

The contractor exam has 55 questions and allows 150 minutes. The salesperson exam has 35 questions and allows 90 minutes. You need 70 percent to pass either one, which is 39 correct for contractors and 25 for salespeople.

Which book do I need for the MHIC contractor exam?

The current PSI bulletin lists the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Maryland Home Improvement Commission, 7th Edition. The Door-to-Door Sales Act is provided at the test site. Make sure you have the 7th Edition and not an older one.

Which book do I need for the MHIC salesperson exam?

Salespeople use the NASCLA Home Improvement Salesperson Reference Guide, Maryland, 6th Edition. The Door-to-Door Sales Act is also provided at the test site and makes up 10 of the 35 questions.

Can I use tabs and highlighting in the MHIC exam?

Yes. PSI allows highlighting, underlining and permanent index tabs, but no handwritten notes or loose papers. Post-it flags and tabs that lift off without tearing the page are not allowed. The proctor decides what is acceptable on test day.

How much does the MHIC exam cost?

The published PSI exam fee is $63, and you pay it again for each retake. License fees are separate and paid to the Commission after you pass. Check the bulletin and the MHIC fee page for current amounts.

What happens if I fail the MHIC exam?

You wait 30 days before your first retake and 60 days before any later retake. You pay the exam fee each time. Many people who fail need more practice finding answers in the book, so timed lookup practice is the best fix.

Do I need experience to get an MHIC contractor license?

Yes. The Commission asks for two years of home improvement or construction experience, related education, or a combination, with proof. You also need to show financial solvency and carry $50,000 in liability insurance.

How long is the MHIC license good for?

A Maryland home improvement license runs for two years from the date the original license is issued. Contractors renew by paying the renewal and Guaranty Fund fees and showing current liability insurance.
