New Mexico NASCLA Exam: How to Get Licensed and Prepare

By Pass Your NASCLA Exam Prep · 12 min read · 10 official sources cited

New Mexico is one of the states that lets a national NASCLA exam stand in for its own trade exam. That can save you a test if you already hold a license elsewhere or plan to work in several states. It does not shortcut the rest of the process. You still need a Qualifying Party certificate, the state Business and Law requirement, a bond and a company license from the Construction Industries Division (CID).

This page walks through how the New Mexico NASCLA exam fits into CID licensing for the GB-98 General Building classification and for electrical work, what each exam looks like, what the state charges, and which of our prep packages lines up with each path. Every rule and fee here is tied to an official source listed at the bottom, and fees can change, so confirm current figures with CID before you pay anything.

Does New Mexico accept the NASCLA exam?

Yes, for both building and electrical work. NASCLA's list of participating agencies shows the New Mexico Construction Industries Division accepting the NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam in place of the state trade exam for the GB-98 license.

On the electrical side, NASCLA's electrical program list shows New Mexico accepting three NASCLA exams: Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician, Journeyman Electrician and Residential Electrician. NASCLA describes the acceptance as replacing the trade exam for the EE-98 license and the journeyman certificate. If you are aiming at a narrower electrical classification such as ER-1, ask CID before you rely on a NASCLA score for it.

There is one condition that catches people. Since January 13, 2023, NASCLA notes that New Mexico also wants a letter of good standing from another state licensing agency showing you have held a license there for at least one year. In practice the NASCLA route suits contractors who are already licensed somewhere else. The state rule behind the waiver, 14.6.3.8 NMAC, lets the bureau chief waive the state written trade exam for a Qualifying Party who passed a NASCLA general construction, electrical or electrical journeyman exam and meets the other requirements.

What NASCLA does not replace: the Business and Law requirement, the experience requirement, the bond, the tax and entity paperwork and workers' compensation coverage. Treat the NASCLA score as one box on a longer checklist.

Who issues contractor and electrical licenses in New Mexico

The Construction Industries Division of the New Mexico Regulation and Licensing Department licenses contractors and certifies journeymen. Its license application page explains the two layers: an individual Qualifying Party (QP) earns a certificate by proving experience and passing exams, and the business then gets a contractor license that rests on that QP.

PSI handles much of the paperwork and testing for CID. Qualifying Party applications, experience affidavits and company license applications go to PSI, and PSI schedules the state exams. CID's page notes that applications must be mailed or delivered by hand; faxed or emailed forms are not accepted.

NASCLA exams run on a separate track. You apply to NASCLA for approval, test with PSI under NASCLA's rules, and then have NASCLA send your passing result to the agency. Keep the two tracks straight in your notes, because each has its own application, its own fees and its own deadlines.

New Mexico contractor license types and which exam each needs

New Mexico sorts work into classifications set out in 14.6.6 NMAC. These are the ones that matter for the NASCLA exams we support:

  • GB-98 General Building: residential and commercial building work, including the GB-2, GF-5, GF-7 and GS scopes. It calls for four years of foreman-level or related trade experience, at least two of them commercial. NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam accepted in place of the trade exam.
  • EE-98 Residential, Commercial and Industrial Electrical Wiring: systems at 5,000 volts nominal or less, plus the listed electrical specialty classes and ER-1 residential work. Four years of experience. NASCLA lists the electrical exams as accepted for EE-98.
  • ER-1 Residential Electrical Wiring: one- and two-family homes and certain ground-floor multifamily buildings, with no hotel or motel work. Two years of experience. Confirm with CID which exam applies.
  • EE-98J Journeyman Residential and Commercial Electrical: an individual certificate requiring four years of experience. The journeyman can work under EE-98, ER-1 and electrical specialty licenses, within that contractor's scope. NASCLA lists its Journeyman Electrician exam as accepted for the journeyman certificate.

How to get a New Mexico contractor license, step by step

The order below follows CID's application instructions with the NASCLA steps added where they fit. Your exact path depends on your classification, so check each step against CID's current forms.

  1. Pick the classification that matches the work your company will do, such as GB-98 or EE-98.
  2. Confirm the Qualifying Party has the required years of experience, earned within the 10 years before the application.
  3. Send the QP application and notarized work experience affidavits to PSI with the published $36 fee. Use one affidavit per person verifying your experience.
  4. Apply for your NASCLA exam at ned.nascla.org and wait for approval before scheduling with PSI.
  5. Pass the NASCLA exam, then order a NASCLA transcript sent to New Mexico.
  6. Get the letter of good standing from another state agency showing at least one year of licensure.
  7. Pass the Contractor's Business and Law exam, or finish a CID-approved Business and Law course instead.
  8. Register the business with the Taxation and Revenue Department, and obtain the entity certificate that applies to your business structure.
  9. Buy the $10,000 contractor license bond and arrange workers' compensation coverage.
  10. Submit the company license application to PSI with the QP certificate or scores, Business and Law proof, bond, tax certificate and fees.

The NASCLA exams New Mexico accepts

The NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam has 115 scored questions and you get 330 minutes. It is open book and computer based, and you need 70 percent to pass (81 correct answers out of the 115 scored questions), which the candidate bulletin states as 81 correct answers. Expect some unscored pretest items as well. The biggest content areas are Procurement and Contracting Requirements at 31 questions, General Requirements at 25 and Site Construction at 15. The bulletin lists a $130 exam fee paid to PSI.

The electrical bulletin covers the three electrical exams. Each has 100 scored questions plus 10 unscored items. The Electrical Contractor exam allows 270 minutes and needs 75 to pass. The Journeyman Electrician exam allows 300 minutes and needs 70. The Residential Electrical Contractor exam allows 270 minutes and needs 70. All three are open book, and the bulletin lists either the 2020 or 2023 NEC as an allowed code edition.

To apply, you create an application at ned.nascla.org. NASCLA's application page lists a $65 application fee, typical processing within seven business days, approval good for one year, and up to three attempts in that year. Each transcript sent to a state agency costs $45 and stays valid for two years.

Tab rules matter on test day. Permanent tabs, highlighting and underlining are allowed. Sticky notes and loose papers are not.

The New Mexico Business and Law exam

Every new Qualifying Party has to satisfy the Contractor's Business and Law requirement, and the NASCLA trade exam does not cover it. CID's application page gives you two ways to meet it: pass the Business and Law exam through PSI, or complete a Business and Law course from an approved vendor. PSI's CID FAQ adds that an active QP adding a classification does not need to repeat it.

NASCLA does not publish a New Mexico edition of its Contractors Guide. Our New Mexico packages pair the NASCLA Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management (14th Edition) with the New Mexico Construction Industries Licensing Act (NMSA Chapter 60, Article 13) and the New Mexico Contractor's License Requirement (NMAC Title 14, Chapter 6, Part 3). The exam is open book, so the goal is to know where things live in those three references.

State exam rules in 14.6.3.8 NMAC set a passing score of 75 percent for CID's own exams and allow no more than two retakes in any 30-day period. CID says a passing score is valid for one year, so time your company application so the score does not lapse.

Experience, bond and insurance requirements

Experience: under 14.6.3.8 NMAC, the Qualifying Party needs the years of experience set for the classification, earned within the 10 years right before the application. Accredited training can count at half a year of credit per year of training, up to half of the total. GB-98 asks for four years with at least two commercial, EE-98 asks for four, and ER-1 asks for two.

Bond: the same rule calls for a $10,000 bond from a corporate surety authorized in New Mexico, both when you first get licensed and at each renewal. The bond form is posted with the PSI contractor forms.

Insurance: CID's application page says every licensed entity must carry workers' compensation coverage, and the rule ties a license's validity to staying compliant with the workers' compensation law.

Entity paperwork: corporations and LLCs submit a certificate from the New Mexico Public Regulation Commission, and limited partnerships and LLPs submit a Secretary of State certificate. Every applicant needs a tax registration certificate from the Taxation and Revenue Department.

New Mexico contractor license costs and fees

These are the published figures as of this writing. CID and PSI update them, so confirm on the PSI fee sheet and the CID fees page before you pay.

  • Qualifying Party application and experience affidavit: $36, made up of a $30 application fee and a $6 certificate fee per classification.
  • Company license application: $30, plus $6 for the certificate and cards.
  • Classification fee: $300 each for GB-98 and EE-98, and $150 for most other single classifications. Several classifications in one category are capped at $300.
  • Journeyman certificate: $75 per classification, new or renewal.
  • NASCLA application: $65. NASCLA transcript to each agency: $45.
  • NASCLA commercial exam fee paid to PSI: $130 per the current commercial bulletin.
  • Contractor license bond: $10,000 in coverage. Your premium depends on the surety.

Reciprocity and working in other states

NASCLA acceptance and reciprocity are not the same thing. New Mexico's only reciprocal journeyman classification is EE-98J, according to CID's application page. It lists agreements with Alaska, Arkansas, Colorado, Idaho, Montana, Nebraska, Oklahoma, South Dakota, Texas and Wyoming, an 8,000-hour experience standard, and a $75 application sent to PSI with a copy of your license from the other state.

The NASCLA score is portable in its own way. Once you pass, the result sits in NASCLA's national database and you can buy a transcript for any participating agency. NASCLA's participating agency list shows which states accept the commercial exam, and many of them also require their own business and law exam.

After you pass: license, renewal and continuing education

Once your QP certificate and company paperwork are in, PSI and CID review the license application. Watch the clock. Under 14.6.3.8 NMAC, a new QP certificate that does not qualify a license within 12 months of the score being reported expires on its own.

For renewal, CID mails a renewal notice at least 30 days before expiration. PSI's FAQ says late fees start at $1 per day for the first 30 days, then equal a full year's fees, and a license more than 90 days past due is cancelled and you have to reapply.

Continuing education depends on the credential. CID's continuing education page says the General Building Bureau has no CE requirement for contractors. Electrical journeymen need 16 hours every three years to renew, eight on code changes and eight on other industry topics.

New Mexico NASCLA exam and license FAQ

Does New Mexico accept the NASCLA commercial exam for a general contractor license?

Yes. CID accepts the NASCLA Commercial General Building Contractor exam in place of the state trade exam for the GB-98 General Building license. You still need the Business and Law requirement, a $10,000 bond, workers' compensation and the company license application.

What is the letter of good standing New Mexico requires for NASCLA candidates?

Since January 13, 2023, NASCLA notes that New Mexico wants a letter from another state's licensing agency showing you have been licensed there for at least one year. Ask your current licensing agency for it early, since it is often the slowest document to arrive.

How much experience do I need for a New Mexico GB-98 license?

Four years of foreman-level or related trade experience, with at least two years on commercial work, all within the 10 years before you apply. Accredited training can cover up to half of the requirement at half credit.

Can I take a course instead of the New Mexico Business and Law exam?

Yes. CID lets you complete a Business and Law course from an approved vendor instead of sitting for the exam. PSI publishes the list of approved courses with its CID forms.

How much does a New Mexico contractor license cost?

Published figures include a $36 QP application, a $30 company application plus a $6 certificate fee, and $300 for GB-98 or EE-98. Add the bond premium, NASCLA's $65 application and $45 transcript, and exam fees. Confirm current amounts with CID and PSI.

Which NASCLA electrical exams does New Mexico accept?

NASCLA lists the Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician, Journeyman Electrician and Residential Electrician exams as accepted in place of the trade exam for the EE-98 license and journeyman certificate. The one-year letter of good standing applies here too.

Is the NASCLA exam open book?

Yes. All the NASCLA exams New Mexico accepts are open book. You may highlight, underline and use permanent tabs, but sticky notes and loose papers are not allowed.

Does New Mexico require continuing education for contractors?

CID says the General Building Bureau has no CE requirement for contractors. Electrical journeymen need 16 hours every three years to renew their certificates.

Does New Mexico have contractor license reciprocity?

For journeymen, only EE-98J is reciprocal, with ten listed states including Texas, Colorado and Oklahoma. For contractor licenses, the NASCLA exam is the main way to skip the state trade test, and the rest of the CID requirements still apply.
