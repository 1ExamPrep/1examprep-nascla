Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Trade Examination for Electrical Contractors is designed for Electrical Contractors, Master Electricians, and Unlimited Electricians. The examination contains 100 scored questions, allows 270 minutes, and requires a passing score of 75%.

The examination content outline includes:

  • Project Design and Management: 8 questions
  • Safety: 9 questions
  • Electrical Theory and Principles: 11 questions
  • General Code Requirements: 17 questions
  • Wiring and Protection: 17 questions
  • Wiring Methods and Materials: 16 questions
  • Equipment for General Use: 13 questions
  • Special Occupancies, Special Equipment, and Special Conditions: 8 questions
  • Communication Systems: 1 question

General Code Requirements and Wiring and Protection are the largest individual categories with 17 questions each. Wiring Methods and Materials follows closely with 16 questions, while Equipment for General Use contributes 13 questions.

Electrical Theory and Principles accounts for 11 questions. Safety contributes 9 questions. Project Design and Management and Special Occupancies, Special Equipment, and Special Conditions each contribute 8 questions.

Together, General Code Requirements, Wiring and Protection, and Wiring Methods and Materials represent 50 of the 100 scored questions. Candidates should therefore become especially comfortable navigating and applying the 2023 National Electrical Code.

Open Book Test

The NASCLA Accredited Electrical Contractor examination is an open-book test.

Candidates bring their permitted references to the examination center. References may be highlighted, underlined, and indexed. Permanent tabs may be used. Temporary tabs such as Post-it notes are not permitted, and references may not contain additional loose or attached papers.

Open-book testing does not remove the need to learn the material. Candidates have approximately 2 minutes and 42 seconds per scored question, which means inefficient searching can consume a significant portion of the testing period.

A productive study process combines technical review with reference navigation. Candidates should learn which books are most useful for different subjects before they begin timed practice.

For example, a question involving conductor ampacity, grounding, branch circuits, raceways, services, feeders, motors, or special occupancies may require the 2023 NEC. A construction-jobsite safety question may lead to OSHA, while energized electrical work and arc-flash safety may require NFPA 70E.

Electrical theory and calculation questions may be supported by Delmar's Standard Textbook of Electricity, Ugly's Electrical References, and Understanding Electrical Theory for NEC Applications. Project-management questions may require a different reference altogether.

Licensing Steps

  1. Determine the North Carolina electrical classification you want. North Carolina issues Limited, Intermediate, Unlimited, and several Special Restricted electrical contracting classifications.
  2. Confirm that you meet the applicable experience requirement. Experience requirements vary according to the North Carolina license classification being requested.
  3. Pass the NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination. North Carolina accepts this NASCLA examination for applicable electrical contracting licensure.
  4. Send your NASCLA examination transcript to North Carolina. NASCLA examination results must be transmitted to the North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors through the NASCLA National Examination Database process.
  5. Complete the North Carolina license application. Submit the application for the electrical contracting classification requested.
  6. Identify the qualified individual. North Carolina electrical contracting licenses require at least one listed qualified individual who is responsible for supervising and directing the electrical work performed under the license.
  7. Submit experience documentation. Provide the documentation required for the classification being requested.
  8. Complete financial requirements when applicable. Intermediate and Unlimited license applications are subject to additional financial-capacity requirements established by the Board.
  9. Complete final Board review. Passing NASCLA satisfies an accepted examination pathway, but the North Carolina license is issued only after the Board approves all applicable licensing requirements.

State Requirements

Electrical contracting in North Carolina is regulated by the North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors.

North Carolina law defines electrical contracting as engaging or offering to engage in the business of installing, maintaining, altering, or repairing electrical work, wiring, devices, appliances, or equipment. A business performing electrical contracting must hold the applicable license classification.

North Carolina issues three principal electrical contracting classifications: Limited, Intermediate, and Unlimited. It also offers several Special Restricted classifications for more limited types of electrical work.

The Limited classification requires at least 3,000 hours of qualifying experience, including at least 2,000 hours of primary experience involving direct installation of electrical wiring and equipment governed by the National Electrical Code.

The Intermediate classification requires at least 5,750 hours of qualifying experience, including at least 5,000 hours of primary electrical experience.

The Unlimited classification requires at least 9,000 hours of qualifying experience, including at least 8,000 hours of primary experience. Unlimited applicants must also provide statements from individuals knowledgeable about the applicant's electrical experience and ability to supervise and direct electrical installation work.

The Unlimited classification permits the licensee to engage in electrical contracting projects without a project-value limit. Limited and Intermediate licenses are subject to the project-value limits established for those classifications.

The North Carolina Board accepts a passing NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination for applicants seeking North Carolina electrical contracting licensure. Applicants who pass this NASCLA examination may apply for Limited, Intermediate, Unlimited, or applicable Special Restricted classifications, subject to the requirements of the classification selected.

North Carolina requires NASCLA-qualified applicants to have their examination transcripts sent to the Board through the NASCLA National Examination Database.

Passing NASCLA does not create a national license and does not automatically produce a North Carolina electrical contractor license. The applicant must still complete the North Carolina licensing application and satisfy the applicable experience, financial, qualified-individual, and other Board requirements.

Test Information and Study Materials

A strong preparation plan should begin with General Code Requirements, Wiring and Protection, and Wiring Methods and Materials. Together, these subjects account for half of the scored examination.

Use the 2023 National Electrical Code regularly throughout preparation. Learn how Chapters 1 through 9 are organized and become comfortable moving between broad requirements and articles covering specific equipment or installation conditions.

General Code Requirements preparation should include terminology, equipment installation, working space, identification, approvals, listing and labeling concepts, and general NEC provisions that affect multiple types of electrical installations.

Wiring and Protection preparation should include branch circuits, feeders, services, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, conductor ampacity, loads, and protection of electrical systems.

Wiring Methods and Materials should include raceways, cables, conductors, boxes, fittings, supports, conduit fill, box fill, installation conditions, and protection from physical damage.

Equipment for General Use accounts for 13 questions. Review motors, transformers, appliances, generators, luminaires, equipment connections, and other commonly encountered electrical equipment.

Electrical Theory and Principles accounts for 11 questions. Practice Ohm's Law, power formulas, series and parallel circuits, voltage, current, resistance, impedance, alternating current, transformer relationships, and other trade calculations.

Use Delmar's Standard Textbook of Electricity, Ugly's Electrical References, and Understanding Electrical Theory for NEC Applications to reinforce calculations and electrical fundamentals.

Safety represents 9 questions. Review OSHA and NFPA 70E as separate but related safety resources. OSHA addresses broad construction-jobsite requirements, while NFPA 70E focuses on electrical workplace hazards and safe electrical work practices.

Study shock hazards, arc-flash hazards, electrical protective equipment, energized work concepts, personal protective equipment, temporary electrical systems, ladders, tools, and other jobsite hazards.

Project Design and Management accounts for 8 questions. Review project planning, documentation, scheduling, resources, coordination, communication, quality, estimating concepts, and risk management.

Special Occupancies, Special Equipment, and Special Conditions also accounts for 8 questions. Become familiar with the NEC chapters that address installations requiring additional or modified electrical rules.

Many NEC questions require candidates to connect general code provisions with a more specific article. Practice following references rather than stopping after locating the first section that appears relevant.

ANSI/ASHRAE/IES 90.1-2022 should be reviewed for commercial energy requirements affecting electrical and lighting systems. Learn the organization of the standard so applicable provisions can be located efficiently.

Reference navigation should be integrated into practice from the beginning. Read the question, identify the principal subject, choose the likely reference, and then begin the lookup.

A question about arc flash should suggest NFPA 70E rather than a random NEC search. A construction fall-protection question should suggest OSHA. A calculation involving electrical theory may be faster to solve with the theory references than by searching code articles.

For NEC questions, learn to use the index strategically. Identify the primary electrical term, find the term in the index, move to the cited article or section, and then verify whether additional provisions apply.

Practice common calculations until you can recognize which formula is required before consulting a reference. This can save time on questions involving current, voltage, resistance, power, motors, transformers, and conductor calculations.

With 100 scored questions in 270 minutes, time management matters. Candidates should answer familiar questions efficiently and avoid allowing one difficult lookup to consume excessive testing time.

As examination day approaches, transition from isolated subject review into mixed-topic practice. Move between NEC code questions, electrical theory, OSHA, NFPA 70E, equipment, special occupancies, project management, and energy requirements.

For North Carolina licensing preparation, candidates should separately become familiar with the classification they intend to request. A passing NASCLA result can support applications for different North Carolina electrical contracting classifications, but each classification retains its own experience and licensing requirements.
