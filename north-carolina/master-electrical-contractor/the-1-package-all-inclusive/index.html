Exam Details

The NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program includes a standardized trade examination for Electrical Contractors, Master Electricians, and Unlimited Electricians. North Carolina became a participating agency in the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program in 2023 through the North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors.

North Carolina accepts the NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination for multiple electrical license classifications. An applicant who has taken and passed this NASCLA examination may apply for a North Carolina SP, Limited, Intermediate, or Unlimited electrical contracting license, provided all other licensing requirements for the chosen classification are satisfied.

The NASCLA electrical examination is designed around the knowledge and responsibilities expected of experienced electrical professionals. Preparation should include electrical theory, National Electrical Code navigation, wiring and protection, wiring methods, safety, equipment, special systems, project management, and related electrical contracting knowledge.

Passing the NASCLA examination does not automatically create a North Carolina electrical license. Applicants must send their NASCLA examination transcripts to the North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors through the NASCLA National Examination Database as part of the licensing application process.

Applicants pursuing the North Carolina Unlimited classification should pay particular attention to the additional documentation required by the Board. The Unlimited classification allows the licensee to engage in electrical contracting projects without a project-value limitation, but applicants must meet the Board's experience and qualification requirements before the license can be issued.

State Requirements

The North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors is the state agency responsible for licensing electrical contractors in North Carolina. The Board recognizes several electrical license classifications, including Limited, Intermediate, Unlimited, and Special Restricted classifications.

North Carolina accepts the NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician examination for applicants seeking the SP, Limited, Intermediate, or Unlimited classification. NASCLA exam results must be transmitted to the Board through the NASCLA National Examination Database as part of the application process.

For the Unlimited classification, North Carolina rules require substantial electrical experience. Applicants must have at least 9,000 hours of qualifying experience, including at least 8,000 hours of primary experience involving direct installation of electrical wiring and equipment governed by the National Electrical Code.

Unlimited applicants must also submit written statements from at least two people who are knowledgeable about the applicant's electrical experience and who can attest to the applicant's ability to supervise and direct electrical wiring or electrical installation work performed by an electrical contracting business.

North Carolina also requires Unlimited classification applicants to provide a bonding ability statement or qualifying line of credit documentation. The Board's NASCLA-qualified-individual application specifies a required bonding ability amount of at least $150,001 for Unlimited applicants.

The Limited classification requires qualifying electrical experience and places limits on the size of individual electrical contracting projects. The Intermediate classification allows larger projects than Limited but also carries its own experience and financial qualification requirements. The Unlimited classification permits electrical contracting projects regardless of project value.

Applicants must be at least 18 years old and satisfy the Board's licensing and qualification rules. Depending on the classification, this may include experience documentation, character references, supervision statements, financial qualifications, license fees, and identification of a qualified individual for the business.

Test Information and Study Materials

A strong NASCLA Master Electrical Contractor study plan should combine technical knowledge with frequent practice using the actual reference books. Because the examination is open book and covers a wide range of electrical subjects, candidates should learn both the material and the location of that material within the references.

The 2023 National Electrical Code should be a central part of your preparation. Practice using the index, locating major articles, reading tables, following cross-references, and identifying how related requirements connect across different NEC sections. Code-navigation practice can be especially useful when questions require you to locate a rule rather than rely on memory.

Electrical theory should be studied alongside code work. Use Delmar's Standard Textbook of Electricity and Understanding Electrical Theory for NEC Applications to reinforce concepts involving voltage, current, resistance, power, circuits, loads, conductors, and common calculations.

Safety study should include both construction jobsite safety and electrical workplace safety. OSHA 29 CFR Part 1926 provides construction-focused safety requirements, while NFPA 70E addresses electrical safety practices in the workplace.

Project-management knowledge should also be part of your preparation. Electrical contractors often need to understand scheduling, coordination, planning, estimating, and project execution in addition to technical electrical work. The included project-management and NASCLA contractor references support this broader preparation.

Use the highlighted and tabbed books during practice sessions rather than saving them for final review. Try to identify the correct reference before searching for an answer. The more often you practice moving from question to reference to section, the more natural the process can become.

North Carolina applicants should also become familiar with the licensing classification they intend to pursue. The NASCLA examination may satisfy the accepted trade-exam pathway, but the license itself depends on meeting the Board's classification-specific requirements. Understanding those requirements early can help you organize documentation while you prepare for the exam.
