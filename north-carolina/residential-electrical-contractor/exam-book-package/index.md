## Licensing Steps

Passing the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination does not automatically issue a North Carolina electrical contractor license. Candidates must complete the licensing process through the North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors.

1. **Identify the SP-SFD classification.** North Carolina accepts the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination for the Special Restricted Single-Family Residential Dwelling license.
2. **Review the experience requirements.** Applicants must satisfy the Board's qualifying experience requirements for the SP-SFD classification.
3. **Prepare the license application.** Applicants must submit the required application and supporting documentation to the Board.
4. **Provide character documentation.** Examination applicants must submit written statements from at least two people attesting to the applicant's good character.
5. **Complete the NASCLA examination process.** Apply for and pass the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination.
6. **Send the NASCLA transcript to North Carolina.** Applicants using the NASCLA pathway must have the examination transcript transmitted to the North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors.
7. **Document qualifying electrical experience.** Provide the experience information needed to establish eligibility for the SP-SFD classification.
8. **Complete the Board's licensing review.** The Board determines whether the applicant satisfies all licensing requirements before issuing the SP-SFD credential.

## State Requirements

Electrical contractor licensing in North Carolina is regulated by the **North Carolina State Board of Examiners of Electrical Contractors**. The Board issues several electrical contractor classifications, including Limited, Intermediate, Unlimited, and special restricted classifications.

For the **SP-SFD Single-Family Detached Residential Dwelling classification**, current Board rules require at least **3,000 hours of qualifying electrical experience**. At least **2,000 hours must be primary experience**, while the remaining qualifying hours may be primary experience, secondary experience, or a combination of both.

North Carolina defines primary experience as work directly involving the installation of electrical wiring and equipment governed by the National Electrical Code or activities directly related to that work. Qualifying primary experience can include work as a journeyman electrician, electrical mechanic, electrical foreman, electrical superintendent, electrical estimator for a licensed contractor, and other qualifying electrical roles recognized by the Board.

Applicants for an electrical contracting examination must also be at least **18 years of age**, submit the required application, and provide written statements from at least two people attesting to good character.

The SP-SFD classification authorizes the licensee to **install, maintain, or repair electrical wiring and devices that are in or on a single-family residential dwelling or ancillary to that dwelling**.

For purposes of the SP-SFD classification, North Carolina defines a single-family residential dwelling as a building or manufactured home designed and used for habitation by one family and not physically attached to another building or structure. Qualifying cabanas, porches, room additions, and ancillary structures can be treated as part of the single-family dwelling when they are designed and used for residential purposes.

Ancillary structures associated with the dwelling can include detached residential garages or carports, household or farm equipment storage buildings, barns, pump houses, electric fences, and yard lighting when the work falls within the authorized residential scope.

The SP-SFD classification is more limited than North Carolina's broader Limited, Intermediate, and Unlimited electrical contractor licenses. Candidates pursuing the residential classification should understand the specific scope of work authorized by the SP-SFD credential before contracting for electrical projects.

North Carolina's acceptance of NASCLA does not remove these state requirements. The NASCLA examination serves as an accepted trade-examination pathway, while experience, character, application, licensing, and scope requirements remain under the authority of the North Carolina Board.
