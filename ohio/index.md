## Does Ohio accept the NASCLA exam?

Not in the way many other states do. Ohio is not on NASCLA's list of agencies that accept the NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors, and it is not listed as a participant in NASCLA's accredited electrical exam program either. So a NASCLA commercial exam score will not stand in for an Ohio trade exam. If you passed it for another state, that result helps you there, not with OCILB.

Where NASCLA does matter in Ohio is the business and law exam. The PSI candidate bulletin names the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Ohio 4th Edition as the reference for that test. NASCLA publishes state editions of the guide that blend its national business content with each state's own laws and rules, and the Ohio edition is the one you want on your desk at the testing center.

That is why people searching for an "Ohio NASCLA exam" usually end up needing the Ohio guide rather than the commercial building exam. If you only remember one thing from this section, make it this: for Ohio, plan on the PSI business and law exam plus your trade exam, and study from the Ohio 4th Edition.

## Who issues contractor licenses in Ohio

State contractor licenses come from the [Ohio Construction Industry Licensing Board](https://com.ohio.gov/divisions-and-programs/industrial-compliance/boards/ohio-construction-industry-licensing-board), which sits in the Division of Industrial Compliance at the Ohio Department of Commerce. The board's authority comes from Ohio Revised Code Chapter 4740. It licenses five commercial trades: electrical, HVAC, plumbing, hydronics and refrigeration.

OCILB is split into specialty sections for plumbing and hydronics, HVAC and refrigeration, and electrical, plus an administrative section that handles matters such as continuing education. Each specialty section reviews applications and authorizes licenses for its trades.

Ohio does not issue a statewide general contractor license through OCILB. General building contractors are usually registered or licensed by the city or county where they work. Columbus, for example, runs its own general contractor registration with a bond and insurance requirement, and its own home improvement contractor license with a separate exam. Local building departments that register contractors must also see a state OCILB license before they issue commercial permits for the five licensed trades. If you are a general contractor, check with the building department in each city where you plan to pull permits.

## Ohio contractor license types and which exam each needs

The PSI bulletin for OCILB lists six exams: the Contractor's Business and Law exam and five trade exams. Each license classification pairs the shared business and law test with its own trade test.

- Electrical Contractor: electrical trade exam plus Business and Law.
- HVAC Contractor: HVAC trade exam plus Business and Law.
- Plumbing Contractor: plumbing trade exam plus Business and Law.
- Hydronics Contractor: hydronics trade exam plus Business and Law.
- Refrigeration Contractor: refrigeration trade exam plus Business and Law.
- Adding a second trade later: per the bulletin, you only need to pass Business and Law once every three years, so a recent pass may carry over. Confirm with PSI or the board before you skip it.

## How to get an Ohio contractor license, step by step

The OCILB testing application lays out the path. Here it is in order, in plain terms.

1. Confirm you qualify: at least 18, a U.S. citizen or legal alien, and five years of experience in the trade right before you apply (or a qualifying alternative).
2. Gather your experience proof: five consecutive years of tax documents plus one supporting item such as permits pulled by your licensed employer, a journeyman card, an approved apprenticeship certificate, or 40 hours of approved live code courses.
3. Complete the OCILB testing application and send it with the $25 published exam application fee.
4. Wait for the board's approval notice. Approval is good for one year.
5. Get a BCI and FBI background check before you test.
6. Register with PSI and schedule both exams, the trade exam and Business and Law.
7. Pass both exams. PSI gives you a pass or fail result after you finish.
8. Send the board your exam results, the $25 published license fee and a certificate of liability insurance showing at least $500,000 in coverage.
9. Assign your license and insurance to a contracting company and list your role there as owner, partner or employee.

## The Ohio contractor business and law exam

PSI Services runs the exam at computer-based test centers. According to the PSI candidate bulletin updated February 7, 2024, the Business and Law exam has 50 scored questions plus 5 unscored pretest questions, a two-hour time limit, and a 70 percent passing score. It is open book, and the reference is the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Ohio 4th Edition.

The content outline is weighted toward money and contracts. Estimating and bidding carries the most items, followed by financial management. Insurance and bonding, contract management, OSHA recordkeeping and safety, personnel rules and tax laws each carry about the same share. Project management, licensing, business organization and lien laws round it out.

The bulletin allows you to bring your own highlighted or tabbed reference, but you may not write notes in it. That rule is exactly why a highlighted and tabbed copy is so useful: you get the speed of marked pages without breaking the no-writing rule. Check the current bulletin before test day, since PSI can update its rules.

Two hours for 55 questions works out to a little over two minutes each. That is enough time if you know where topics live in the book and too little if you are flipping from the front cover every time.

## Scheduling, retakes and test day

Once OCILB approves you and notifies PSI, you have one year to schedule and sit for the exam. PSI takes registrations online at any hour and by phone. The published PSI exam fee is $69 per registration, it is not refundable, and every retake needs a new fee.

If you fail, you must wait 60 days before trying again, and the bulletin caps you at five retests in a year. Ohio law adds that after five failed attempts you must reapply before testing again. To keep your fee when you need to move an appointment, cancel or reschedule at least two days ahead. Arrive 15 minutes early with a current, signed photo ID that matches the name you registered under. A late arrival forfeits the fee.

## Ohio experience, insurance and background requirements

Ohio Revised Code 4740.06 sets the core rules. You need five years as a tradesperson in the trade you are licensing, immediately before you apply. A registered Ohio engineer with three years of construction business experience in the trade can qualify instead, and the testing application also accepts five years as an Ohio government inspector in the trade. Veterans can attach a DD-214 and related training records.

The testing application spells out how to prove experience. You need five consecutive years of W-2s or Schedule C returns, plus one of the following: at least one permit per year for five years pulled by your licensed employer (sewer, water, gas service and drain cleaning permits do not count), an active journeyman card, a state or U.S. Department of Labor apprenticeship certificate, or 40 hours of non-duplicated code courses from an OCILB-approved provider taken live or virtually.

For insurance, the board's application calls for contractor liability coverage of at least $500,000, and the statute says you may carry it under only one contracting company name. Expect a BCI and FBI background check after the board approves your application.

## Reciprocity and out-of-state contractors

Ohio has trade reciprocity agreements posted on the OCILB site. The Kentucky agreement, for instance, covers electrical and HVAC licenses. A Kentucky master electrician and contractor license qualifies only if the holder originally tested for it, and grandfathered licenses are excluded. You still need a separate application and a $25 fee for each trade, a $500,000 liability certificate, and a contracting company to assign the license to.

Ohio law also gives a route for licensed contractors from other states. Under ORC 4740.06(C) you show at least five building permits or similar authorizations issued under your other license, at least one tax return reflecting income from that license, and employment with a contracting company registered in Ohio. Because agreement terms differ by state and trade, ask OCILB which path fits your license.

## After you pass: license, renewal and continuing education

Once you pass, send your results, the license fee and your insurance certificate to OCILB, then assign the license to your contracting company. Ohio law calls for the board to authorize a license within 90 days of the application filing date for qualified applicants who pass. You can confirm your status through the board's online licensee lookup.

Ohio contractor licenses renew every year on the anniversary of the original license. You can renew through the state eLicense system up to 90 days before expiration with the renewal fee and proof of continuing education. No new exam is needed. ORC 4740.04 requires ten hours of continuing education per year, no matter how many OCILB licenses you hold. Take courses from a board-approved provider and keep your certificates.

## Ohio NASCLA exam and license FAQ

### Does Ohio require a general contractor license?

Ohio's state board licenses only five commercial trades: electrical, HVAC, plumbing, hydronics and refrigeration. General contractors are usually registered or licensed by the city or county where they work, such as the City of Columbus. Check with each local building department before you bid.

### Is the Ohio contractor business and law exam open book?

Yes. The PSI bulletin lists the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Ohio 4th Edition as the reference. You may bring a highlighted or tabbed copy, but no handwritten notes inside it.

### How many questions are on the Ohio business and law exam?

The PSI bulletin lists 50 scored questions plus 5 unscored pretest questions, with two hours to finish. You need 70 percent to pass.

### Can I use the NASCLA commercial exam to get licensed in Ohio?

No. Ohio is not on NASCLA's list of agencies that accept the commercial general building exam. Ohio candidates take PSI's trade and business and law exams instead, and the business and law exam uses the NASCLA Ohio guide.

### How much experience do I need for an OCILB license?

Ohio law requires five years in the trade right before you apply. A registered Ohio engineer with three years of construction business experience in the trade can qualify another way. The testing application lists the documents that prove your experience.

### What happens if I fail the Ohio business and law exam?

You wait 60 days, register again with PSI and pay a new exam fee. The bulletin limits you to five retests in a year, and Ohio law requires a new application after five failed attempts.

### How much insurance do Ohio contractors need?

The OCILB application requires a certificate of liability insurance showing at least $500,000 in coverage. You need to keep that coverage in force to keep your license.

### How many continuing education hours do Ohio contractors need?

Ohio law requires ten hours of continuing education per year for license renewal, no matter how many OCILB licenses you hold. Licenses renew every year.

### Which edition of the NASCLA guide does Ohio use?

The current PSI bulletin names the Ohio 4th Edition of the NASCLA Contractor's Guide to Business, Law and Project Management. Older Ohio editions do not match the exam reference, so bring the 4th Edition.
