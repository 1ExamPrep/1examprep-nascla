## Open Book Test

The NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination is an **open book test**. NASCLA identifies its Residential Electrical Contractor examination as one of the open-book electrical trade examinations offered through the Accredited Electrical Examination Program.

Open-book testing makes reference familiarity an important part of preparation. Candidates should develop a repeatable process for identifying the subject of a question, selecting the correct reference, locating the applicable provision, table, definition, formula, or safety requirement, and then applying that information accurately.

The **National Electrical Code, NEC, 2023** should receive substantial attention. Candidates should become comfortable using Article 100 definitions, the index, article organization, tables, exceptions, and cross-references. Residential preparation should include services, feeders, branch circuits, receptacles, lighting outlets, conductor sizing, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, wiring methods, appliances, motors, equipment, and special systems.

Electrical theory should be studied alongside code navigation. Review voltage, current, resistance, power, Ohm's law, series and parallel circuits, alternating-current fundamentals, electrical loads, motors, transformers, and practical calculations. Understanding the principle behind a problem can help candidates determine which formula or code provision applies.

OSHA 29 CFR Part 1926 supports preparation involving construction-industry safety, while NFPA 70E focuses on electrical safe-work practices and electrical hazards. NFPA 72 supports fire alarm and signaling-system study. The International Residential Code provides broader residential construction context.

Ugly's Electrical References and Understanding Electrical Theory for NEC Applications support formulas, calculations, and electrical concepts. ANSI Z535.4-2023 addresses product safety signs and labels, while the NASCLA Contractors Guide supports contractor business, law, and project-management study.

## Licensing Steps

Passing the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractor examination does not automatically issue a South Carolina Residential Electrician license. Applicants must complete the licensing process through the South Carolina Residential Builders Commission.

1. **Review the Residential Electrician classification.** Confirm that the South Carolina residential specialty electrical license matches the work you intend to perform.
2. **Meet the experience requirement.** Applicants must have at least one year of Commission-approved experience under the supervision of a licensee in the trade before being eligible for examination-based licensure.
3. **Complete the application process.** Submit the required application and supporting experience documentation to the Residential Builders Commission.
4. **Use the NASCLA technical-exam waiver when eligible.** Applicants who passed the NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractors exam within the previous three years may use that examination in place of the South Carolina technical exam.
5. **Submit the NASCLA transcript.** Include a copy of the applicable NASCLA examination transcript with the South Carolina application.
6. **Complete the Business Management and Law examination.** The NASCLA waiver applies to the technical portion and does not eliminate South Carolina's Business Management and Law requirement.
7. **Complete any remaining licensing documentation.** Provide additional records or forms requested by the Residential Builders Commission.
8. **Receive the license before performing regulated work.** The Commission determines whether the applicant has satisfied all requirements before issuing the Residential Electrician license.

## State Requirements

The **South Carolina Residential Builders Commission** regulates residential builders, residential specialty contractors, and other residential construction license categories. Residential Electrician is one of the specialty contractor classifications administered through the Commission.

Before an applicant can qualify for examination-based residential specialty licensure, South Carolina requires at least **one year of Commission-approved experience under the supervision of a licensee** in the trade for which the applicant is applying.

The Commission verifies qualifying experience through documentation associated with the application. Residential electrical experience may include hands-on installation and service work involving residential electrical systems, service equipment, branch circuits, wiring, electrical devices, and related residential electrical work.

South Carolina's standard examination process includes two parts. The Residential Electrical technical examination contains 60 multiple-choice questions and requires a passing score of 73.3%. The Business Management and Law examination contains 50 multiple-choice questions and is a separate requirement.

Applicants who have passed the **NASCLA Residential Electrical Contractors exam within the previous three years** may obtain a waiver of the technical examination. A copy of the NASCLA exam transcript must be included with the application.

The NASCLA waiver does not waive the Business Management and Law examination. Candidates should therefore prepare for the state business requirement separately from the NASCLA residential electrical trade examination.

South Carolina also recognizes other technical-exam waiver pathways for qualifying applicants, including certain licensees from North Carolina and Mississippi and certain qualifying electrical certifications. These are separate from the NASCLA pathway.

A South Carolina Residential Electrician license is a residential specialty contractor credential. It is not the same as the commercial electrical contractor classification regulated by the South Carolina Contractors' Licensing Board. Applicants planning to perform commercial electrical contracting should review the separate commercial licensing requirements applicable to that work.

Licensees must perform electrical work only within the scope authorized by the credential held and comply with applicable permitting, inspection, building-code, electrical-code, and local business requirements.
