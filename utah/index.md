By Pass Your NASCLA Exam Prep · 12 min read · 8 official sources cited

If you want a commercial general contractor license in Utah, the NASCLA exam can do real work for you. Utah stopped requiring trade exams for its general contractor classifications, but the Division of Professional Licensing still asks you to prove two years of construction experience. A passing score on the NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors is one of the accepted ways to meet that experience requirement.

## Does Utah accept the NASCLA exam?

Yes. NASCLA lists the Utah Division of Professional Licensing, through its Construction Trades Bureau, as a participating agency for the commercial exam. According to that listing, Utah has not required trade exams since May 2019 but will accept a passing NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors to satisfy the two-year experience requirement for general contractors.

That last point is what makes Utah different from most states. In many states the NASCLA exam replaces a state trade test. In Utah there is no state trade test to replace. Instead, your NASCLA result stands in for the experience you would otherwise have to document. That can matter if your paid construction hours are hard to prove, or if your experience came from work that is difficult to verify.

Passing NASCLA does not finish the job. The Division's general contractor page still requires you to meet everything a specialty contractor must meet, plus the Utah Business and Law exam or one of its listed alternatives. Your business also needs the insurance and financial responsibility the Division requires before the license goes active.

If you already have two years of paid construction work you can document, you may not need NASCLA at all. It is one route among several, and the right choice depends on your records. See the [NASCLA participating agencies list](https://www.nascla.org/page/ParticipatingAgencies) and the Division's [general contractor license page](https://commerce.utah.gov/dopl/contracting/apply-for-a-license/general-contractor/) for the current wording.

## Who issues Utah general contractor licenses

Contractor licenses in Utah are issued by the Division of Professional Licensing (DOPL), part of the Utah Department of Commerce. Its Construction Trades Bureau handles contractor classifications, applications, renewals and continuing education. The Division's contracting pages at [commerce.utah.gov](https://commerce.utah.gov/dopl/contracting/apply-for-a-license/general-contractor/) are the place to check current rules and download forms.

The license is issued to the business, not to you personally. Your application names the legal entity, its owners, the classification you want, and the qualifier whose background supports the license. Keep the business name identical across your application, your registration with the Utah Division of Corporations, and your insurance certificate. The Division's insurance instructions specifically ask that any DBA shown on the certificate be owned by the business entity on the application.

Electrical and plumbing contracting also run through DOPL, with added rules. The general contractor page notes that electrical contractors need a master electrician qualifier and plumbing contractors need a master plumber qualifier. Those trades follow a separate path from the B100 route described here.

## Utah contractor license types and which exam each needs

The Division groups its general contractor licenses into a few headline classifications. The ones you will see most often are B100 General Building Contractor, R100 Residential and Small Commercial Contractor, and E100 General Engineering Contractor. The same page also lists general and residential plumbing (P200, P201) and general and residential electrical (E200, E201).

For broad commercial building work, B100 is the classification to study. It is also the one that lines up best with the NASCLA commercial exam, since that exam covers multi-trade commercial projects. Read the official scope before you apply so you know the classification matches the work you plan to bid.

Here is how the exams fit together for the general contractor classifications, based on the Division's current page:

- Trade exam: none required by Utah for general contractor classifications.
- Experience: two years or 4,000 hours of paid construction work, or one of the alternatives, which include passing the NASCLA commercial exam.
- Business and Law: the Utah Business and Law exam, unless you qualify for one of the listed exceptions.
- Specialty contractor licenses: the Division's FAQ states that specialty classifications require no experience.

## How to get a Utah general contractor license, step by step

The order below follows the Division's general contractor page and the NASCLA process. Your path may skip a step if you qualify through experience instead of NASCLA.

1. Pick your classification. Compare B100, R100 and E100 against the work you intend to do.
2. Set up the business. Choose the entity type and register it with the Utah Division of Corporations so the legal name is settled before you apply.
3. Choose your qualifier. This is the person whose experience and exam results support the license.
4. Decide how you will meet the experience requirement: documented paid hours, a construction management degree, a Utah professional engineer license, prior time as a Utah license qualifier, or a passing NASCLA result.
5. If you are using NASCLA, apply for the commercial exam through NASCLA at ned.nascla.org, complete its pre-approval step, then schedule and pass the exam.
6. Send your NASCLA transcript to the Division through the NASCLA National Examination Database.
7. Pass the Utah Business and Law exam through ProV, unless an exception applies, and keep a copy of your result.
8. Line up general liability insurance and financial responsibility, with DOPL named as certificate holder.
9. Submit the contractor application online or on the paper form, with your exam results and supporting records.
10. Wait for the license before you bid or perform work that requires one.

## The NASCLA commercial exam itself

The NASCLA Accredited Examination for Commercial General Building Contractors has 115 scored questions and a 330-minute time limit. You need 70 percent to pass (81 correct answers out of the 115 scored questions). The exam may also contain a few unscored pretest questions, so do not be thrown if the count on screen looks higher.

It is an open-book exam, and that changes how you should study. You bring your own approved references. The testing center does not supply them. The reference list covers project and jobsite management, structural concrete under ACI 318-19, reinforcing bar placement, precast erection, post-tensioning, steel deck, steel joists, wood truss bracing, masonry, excavation, carpentry, roofing, accessibility under ICC A117.1, the International Building Code, OSHA's construction rules in 29 CFR Part 1926, and stormwater planning.

You apply through NASCLA, not through Utah. Create an account at ned.nascla.org, complete the pre-approval and payment steps, and then schedule your seat. Your scores live in NASCLA's National Examination Database, and from there you can send a transcript electronically to the Division. NASCLA's [commercial exam page](https://nascla.org/nascla-commercial-exam) explains the transcript process.

Because the result is stored nationally, the same score can later support applications in other participating states, subject to each state's own rules. Retake rules and fees are set by NASCLA and its testing provider, so check the current candidate bulletin before you book.

## The Utah Business and Law exam

Separate from NASCLA, most new Utah general contractor applicants must pass the Utah Business and Law exam. The NASCLA commercial exam does not cover this requirement. The Division's exam information page names ProV as its contractor exam administrator and says you do not need pre-approval to sit for it.

The Division lists ProV as its exam administrator, with in-person testing centers and an online option for its contractor exams. Its exam page also describes a retake schedule with waiting periods between attempts. Check ProV's Business and Law handbook for the current testing options, retake rules, references and ID requirements before you book, pay the exam fee directly to ProV, and do not submit your license application until you have passed.

The study reference is the NASCLA Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Utah 4th Edition. NASCLA's store lists it alongside the Utah licensing agency and Prov as the testing provider. The book is organized around business planning and startup, day-to-day operations such as estimating, contracts, scheduling and safety, and administrative topics such as financial management, tax basics and lien laws.

The Division's page lists exceptions to the Business and Law exam. One is holding an active license in good standing in another state, district or territory for at least one year in a similar scope. Another applies to people who were qualifiers on a Utah contractor license before May 9, 2017. If either fits you, confirm with the Division before skipping the exam. Details are on the [DOPL exam information page](https://commerce.utah.gov/dopl/contracting/exam-information/).

## Experience, insurance and financial requirements in Utah

For the general contractor classifications, the Division asks for two years in the construction industry, which it describes as at least 4,000 hours of paid work experience gained at any point in your life. The experience is self-certified on the application. The Division's FAQ adds that military construction work, owner experience and supervised construction education programs can count.

If you cannot meet the hours, the general contractor page lists alternatives. You can qualify through at least two years as a qualifier on a current or past Utah contractor license, a two-year or four-year construction management degree, a Utah professional engineer license, or a passing NASCLA commercial exam result.

Every active license must carry general liability insurance and financial responsibility, according to the Division's FAQ. The certificate must name the Division of Professional Licensing as certificate holder and must show coverage that fits your scope of work. You will provide an active policy each time you renew.

Utah sets the dollar minimums for liability coverage by administrative rule, and those minimums have been under revision. Rather than rely on a figure that may be out of date, ask your insurance agent and the Division for the current required limits before you buy a policy. Inactive licenses are exempt from the insurance and financial responsibility requirements, per the FAQ.

## Reciprocity and moving between states

The Division's FAQ is direct about this: Utah does not have reciprocity. What it offers instead is licensure by endorsement, and the Division publishes a state-by-state contractor endorsement map on its FAQ page so you can see where your current license may help.

Holding another state's license can also help with the Business and Law exam. As noted above, one year of active licensure in another jurisdiction in a similar scope is listed as an alternative to that exam.

Your NASCLA score is portable in a different way. It sits in the National Examination Database, and you can send a transcript to any participating state that accepts the commercial exam. Each of those states will still apply its own experience, financial, insurance and business law rules. If you plan to work in several western states, passing NASCLA once and keeping the transcript current can save you from sitting for multiple trade exams. Check the [NASCLA participating agencies list](https://www.nascla.org/page/ParticipatingAgencies) for each state's terms.

## How to study for an open-book exam

Open book does not mean easy. With 115 questions in 330 minutes, you have under three minutes per question, and the library runs to thousands of pages. The candidates who do well are the ones who can find an answer fast, not the ones who read every chapter.

Start by splitting your books by subject: project management, safety, codes and accessibility, site work, concrete, structural systems, masonry, carpentry, roofing and building envelope. Then practice with sample questions. For each one, name the topic, pick the book, and find the page using the tabs, index or table of contents. Time yourself.

When you miss a question, figure out why. Did you choose the wrong book, misread a term, or just not know the material? Each cause needs a different fix. Read highlighted passages in context so you understand the rule around the number.

Give the Business and Law guide its own block of time. It covers different ground, from lien law to payroll taxes, and it rewards steady review more than cramming.

## After you pass: application, renewal and continuing education

Once you have your exam results, insurance certificate and records ready, apply through the Division's online portal or with the paper contractor application. Licensing fees are processing fees and are not refundable, even if you withdraw, so make sure everything is complete before you pay.

Utah contractor licenses renew every two years. According to the Division's FAQ, they expire on November 30 of each odd-numbered year. That means a license issued late in a cycle may come up for renewal sooner than you expect.

You need 6 hours of approved continuing education each licensing period. At least 3 of those hours must be core, the rest may be core or professional, and no more than 3 hours may be completed online. The full rules are in Utah Administrative Rule R156-55a, linked from the Division's [continuing education page](https://commerce.utah.gov/dopl/contracting/continuing-education/).

If you move your license to inactive status, you skip the insurance and financial responsibility requirements while inactive. To reactivate, the Division asks for a questionnaire, proof of liability insurance and financial responsibility, and proof of continuing education.

## Utah NASCLA exam and license FAQ

### Does Utah require a trade exam for a general contractor license?

No. NASCLA's participating agency listing says Utah stopped requiring trade exams in May 2019. You still need to meet the experience requirement and, in most cases, pass the Utah Business and Law exam.

### How does the NASCLA exam help with a Utah license?

A passing NASCLA commercial exam result is one of the accepted alternatives to the two-year, 4,000-hour paid experience requirement for general contractors. Send the transcript to the Division through the NASCLA National Examination Database.

### Do I still need the Utah Business and Law exam if I pass NASCLA?

Yes, unless you qualify for an exception such as one year of active licensure in a similar scope in another state. The NASCLA exam covers technical construction knowledge, not Utah business and law.

### Who gives the Utah Business and Law exam?

The Division names ProV as its contractor exam administrator, with testing centers and an online option. ProV's Business and Law handbook has the current scheduling details.

### Which book do I need for the Utah Business and Law exam?

Study the NASCLA Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, Utah 4th Edition. Check ProV's current handbook to confirm the reference list before test day.

### How many times can I retake the Utah Business and Law exam?

The Division's exam page describes a limited number of attempts with waiting periods between retakes. Confirm the current retake rules for the Business and Law exam in ProV's handbook before you schedule.

### Does Utah have contractor license reciprocity?

No. The Division states that Utah does not have reciprocity but offers licensure by endorsement, with a state-by-state map on its FAQ page.

### How often do Utah contractor licenses renew?

Every two years, with licenses expiring on November 30 of odd-numbered years. You need 6 hours of approved continuing education per period, with at least 3 hours of core content.

### Is the NASCLA exam open book?

Yes. You bring your own approved references to the test center, which does not provide them. Highlighted and permanently tabbed books help you find answers faster.
