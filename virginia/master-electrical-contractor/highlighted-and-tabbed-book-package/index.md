## State Requirements

The Virginia Board for Contractors, operating through the Department of Professional and Occupational Regulation, regulates contractor businesses and individual tradesmen working in electrical and other construction trades.

Virginia contractor firms are licensed as Class A, Class B, or Class C contractors. The contractor license applies to the business entity or sole proprietorship rather than functioning as an individual tradesman license. The business also carries classifications or specialties that determine the type of work it may perform.

Virginia requires contractor applicants for Class A, Class B, and Class C licenses to complete eight hours of Board-approved pre-license education. The pre-license course is a business-focused requirement addressing Virginia statutes, regulations, and contractor responsibilities. It is separate from electrical trade preparation.

For Class A and Class B contractor applicants, the required pre-license education must be completed by a member of responsible management or the designated employee. For Class C applicants, a member of responsible management must complete the course.

Virginia also requires every licensed contractor to identify a qualified individual for each specialty on the contractor license. For the Electrical Contractors specialty, Virginia regulations identify a Master Electrician tradesman license issued by the Board for Contractors as the required qualification for the qualified individual.

This distinction is important for electrical professionals. The Master Electrician license is an individual credential, while the Electrical Contractors specialty is attached to the licensed contracting business. A person can therefore encounter separate requirements related to individual master licensure and contractor-business licensing.

Virginia participates in the NASCLA Accredited Electrical Examination Program. NASCLA passing results may be transmitted to participating agencies through the NASCLA National Examination Database. The regulatory agency then pairs the examination result with the candidate's licensing application and evaluates whether the applicant meets its separate licensing requirements.

Passing the NASCLA Electrical Contractor examination does not create a national license and does not automatically issue a Virginia license. Applicants must still complete the Virginia licensing process, satisfy the state's application requirements, and meet the qualification standards associated with the credential they seek.

Begin with the 2023 National Electrical Code. Electrical theory should be studied through active calculations. Review voltage, current, resistance, power, circuit relationships, motors, transformers, and other electrical principles. For calculation problems, develop a repeatable process: identify known information, determine what must be solved, choose the appropriate formula, calculate carefully, and then determine whether a Code requirement affects the final answer.

Safety preparation should include both OSHA Part 1926 and NFPA 70E. Learning how each reference is organized helps candidates distinguish construction-safety requirements from electrical workplace-safety requirements and locate relevant information more efficiently.

Project-management material should also remain part of the study plan. Electrical contractors work within projects involving planning, communication, scheduling, coordination, risk, documentation, and business responsibilities. These subjects complement technical electrical knowledge and can be important to professional contractor preparation.

Timed reference exercises can help candidates use the highlighted and tabbed format effectively. Select a practice question, identify the subject, navigate to the correct reference section, find the relevant information, and measure how long the process takes. Repeated practice can reveal which subjects need additional attention and which references require greater familiarity.
