Washington DC NASCLA Exam Guide for Journeyman Electricians

By Pass Your NASCLA Exam Prep · 11 min read · 8 official sources cited

The District of Columbia is not a state, so it does not have a state licensing board. Electricians in the District are licensed by the DC Board of Industrial Trades, which sits inside the Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection (DLCP). Since February 2021, the District has accepted NASCLA's accredited electrical exams, which gives you a national, open-book exam you can take at a PSI site and then use toward your DC journeyman license.

This guide walks you through what the District accepts, which license you need, the experience the DC Code asks for, the published fees, the Maryland and Virginia reciprocity route, and how to get ready for the NASCLA Journeyman Electrician exam. Does Washington DC accept the NASCLA exam?

Yes, for electricians. NASCLA lists the DC Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection as an accepting agency in its Accredited Electrical Examination Program, effective February 2021. The District accepts two of the three NASCLA electrical exams: the Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician exam and the Journeyman Electrician exam. The Residential Electrical Contractor exam is not on the District's list.

Accepting means the District can count a passing NASCLA result where it would otherwise require its own trade exam. It does not mean the exam is the license. You still apply to the Board of Industrial Trades, prove your hours or education, finish the required code class, and pay the District's fees. DLCP lists NASCLA accreditation among the documents electricians can upload with an application, so the NASCLA result becomes one piece of your file.

The District does not appear in NASCLA's commercial building program, so there is no NASCLA path to a DC general contractor credential. This page focuses on the journeyman electrician exam, which is the DC exam we carry prep for. If you are aiming for a DC master license instead, the District also accepts the NASCLA Master exam, but confirm the master requirements with the Board first.

Who issues electrician licenses in the District of Columbia

The Board of Industrial Trades at DLCP handles licensing for electricians, along with several other regulated trades in the District. For electricians, the Board issues apprentice, journeyman, master and contractor licenses, plus a short-term temporary journeyman license that runs up to 60 days.

DLCP's office is at 1100 4th Street SW, and NASCLA lists the agency's phone as (202) 442-4335. Applications and renewals are filed online through the District's professional licensing portal, which is linked from the Board's page. The legal rules for electricians are in the DC Code, including section 47-2853.92, which sets out who can qualify as a journeyman.

Because the District works as both city and state, there is no separate county or city electrical license layered on top. Permits and inspections are a different matter, so expect to deal with DC permitting on jobs even after you hold your license.

DC electrician license types and which exam each needs

DLCP sorts electricians into four license levels. Each one has a different entry route, and only two of them involve a NASCLA exam.

  • Apprentice: no exam. You need employment verification or proof you are enrolled in a training program.
  • Journeyman (all types): DLCP's published requirements list 4 years and 8,000 work hours. You qualify by exam, by a waiver for completing a nationally certified apprenticeship, or by reciprocity. The NASCLA Journeyman Electrician exam is accepted for the exam route.
  • Master (all types): DLCP's published requirements list 8 years and 16,000 work hours, with licensure by exam or reciprocity. The NASCLA Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician exam is accepted.
  • Contractor (all types): a business license level. DLCP asks for proof of business registration or good standing, a designated DC-licensed master tradesperson, and an electrical bond.
  • Temporary journeyman: a short license of up to 60 days, listed by DLCP at the same fee as a full journeyman license.

How to get a DC journeyman electrician license, step by step

Here is the order most applicants follow when they use the NASCLA exam. Check the Board's page before each step in case a form or requirement has changed.

  1. Confirm your route. Decide whether you qualify by apprenticeship hours, by an electrical engineering degree plus field time, by a national apprenticeship waiver, or by Maryland or Virginia reciprocity. Only the exam route needs NASCLA.
  2. Collect proof of experience. DLCP wants employment verification letters signed by the master you worked under, showing how long you worked and what you did. Tax records may also be requested.
  3. Take the required code class. The DC Code asks journeyman applicants to complete a class on the District's electrical code (DCMR Title 12-C) or an equivalent code within two years before applying.
  4. Apply to NASCLA. Submit your exam application at ned.nascla.org and pay NASCLA's $65 application fee. NASCLA says applications are usually processed within 7 business days.
  5. Schedule with PSI. Once approved, you get a NASCLA candidate ID by email and a link to book a seat. You can sit at any PSI site that offers the exam.
  6. Pass the exam, then link and send your result. Create a NASCLA National Examination Database account to link your score, and buy a transcript for DLCP ($45 per agency, sent electronically).
  7. Apply to the Board. File online with a 2 inch by 2 inch passport-style photo, a government ID, your experience letters, the NASCLA documentation and a card for the $65 application fee.
  8. Pay the license fee once approved. The journeyman license fee is published at $110 for a term of up to two years.

The NASCLA Journeyman Electrician exam in Washington DC

NASCLA's electrical exams are given by PSI. According to the PSI candidate bulletin, the Journeyman Electrician exam has 100 scored questions plus 10 unscored pretest questions, you get 300 minutes, and the minimum passing score is 70. It is open book, and PSI hands out a diagram and blueprint packet at the test site. A silent, non-printing, non-programmable calculator is allowed.

The bulletin's reference list for the journeyman exam is the 2020 or 2023 National Electrical Code (or NEC Handbook), OSHA 29 CFR 1926 and 29 CFR 1910 (2024), NFPA 70E (2024), NFPA 72 (2022), Ugly's Electrical References (2023) and Understanding Electrical Theory for NEC Applications. Code questions are written only to the listed NEC editions, so bring one of those two.

The content outline puts the most weight on wiring and protection (18 questions), wiring methods and materials (16) and general code requirements (15). Electrical theory, equipment for general use, safety, special occupancies, testing and troubleshooting, and plans and specifications make up the rest.

Your approved NASCLA application is good for one year from approval, and you may test up to three times in that year. If you have not passed after three tries, you wait until the year runs out and then reapply. The PSI exam fee is paid after NASCLA approves you; check PSI or NASCLA for the current amount.

Is there a DC business and law exam?

No separate business and law exam shows up for DC journeyman electricians in DLCP's published requirements, and NASCLA does not publish a District edition of its Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management. That makes the journeyman route simpler than in many states: the trade exam, the code class and your documented experience carry most of the weight.

What the District does require is the code class on its own electrical rules. The DC Code ties it to DCMR Title 12-C, or an equivalent code, taken within two years before you apply. Keep the completion certificate with your application file. If you later move up to a contractor license, expect business-side requirements such as registration, a designated master and an electrical bond, and check with the Board for anything else it asks of contractors.

DC electrician license requirements: experience and education

Section 47-2853.92 of the DC Code gives three ways to meet the journeyman experience standard. The first is working as an apprentice electrician for at least 8,000 hours over at least four years. The second is a degree in electrical engineering from an accredited college or university plus at least two years of practical electrical work, certified by a licensed master electrician. The third lets the Board accept other experience, or a mix of schooling and experience, that it finds equivalent.

On top of experience, the statute requires the code class described above and any added proof the Board asks for in a given specialty. A separate subsection directs the Board to accept a certificate from a national certifying organization, which can include recognized trade groups and labor unions, when it shows you passed that group's exam, hold its journeyman designation and have not been disciplined. DLCP describes this as a waiver for finishing a nationally certified apprenticeship, backed by a completion certificate or transcript.

For the experience letters, DLCP asks that they be signed by the master you worked for and spell out your length of employment and the work you did. Weak or vague letters are a common reason files stall, so ask your employers for detail.

DC electrician license fees

These are the published figures on the Board of Industrial Trades page when we checked. Fees change, so confirm on that page before you pay. DLCP notes that application fees are not refunded, even if you withdraw or are found not qualified.

  • Application fee: $65.
  • Journeyman license: $110 for up to two years. Apprentice license: $110. Master and contractor licenses: $120 each.
  • Temporary journeyman license: $110 for up to 60 days.
  • Renewal: journeyman $110, apprentice $110, master $155, contractor $180. Late renewal adds $50.
  • Duplicate license or records verification: $30 each.
  • NASCLA side: $65 NASCLA exam application and $45 for each transcript sent to an agency, plus the PSI exam fee.

Reciprocity: Maryland and Virginia electricians

If you already hold a journeyman or master electrician license in Maryland or Virginia, DLCP offers a reciprocity route that skips the exam. The home license must have been earned by examination and must be current, active and in good standing. You ask your home board for a letter of good standing or a license verification, and you upload it with your DC application. DLCP's older checklist adds that the letter should say plainly that you got the license by exam.

Reciprocity and NASCLA are separate tracks. Reciprocity rests on a license you already hold; NASCLA acceptance rests on an exam you pass. If you are licensed somewhere other than Maryland or Virginia, the NASCLA Journeyman exam is the most direct way to meet DC's exam requirement.

The NASCLA result is portable too. Other agencies in the electrical program accept the journeyman exam, including Virginia, Kentucky, New Mexico, Tennessee and Utah, each with its own conditions. NASCLA says passing results do not expire, and you can buy a transcript for each agency you apply to.

How to prepare for the DC journeyman electrician exam, and which package fits

Because the exam is open book, speed with the references matters as much as what you know. Spend most of your time in the NEC: Article 100 definitions, the index, branch circuits, feeders, services, conductor ampacity, overcurrent protection, grounding and bonding, and wiring methods. Then add theory problems, the two OSHA books, NFPA 70E for safe work practices and NFPA 72 for fire alarm questions. Practice the same routine on every question: name the topic, pick the book, find the rule or table, and check it.

After you pass: your DC license and renewal

Once PSI posts your result, it can take a few business days to link to your NASCLA account. Buy the transcript for DLCP, then finish your Board application with your experience letters and code class certificate. The Board reviews the file and decides whether you meet every requirement before it issues the license.

Every DC electrician license expires on November 30 of an odd-numbered year, no matter when it was issued. That means a license issued late in a cycle may run well under two years before the first renewal. Renew through the District's online portal; the published journeyman renewal fee is $110, and a late renewal adds $50.

DLCP's pages we reviewed do not spell out continuing education hours for renewal. Check with the Board of Industrial Trades before your renewal date so you are not caught short.

Washington DC NASCLA exam and license FAQ

Does Washington DC accept the NASCLA Journeyman Electrician exam?

Yes. NASCLA lists DC's Department of Licensing and Consumer Protection as accepting both the Journeyman Electrician and the Electrical Contractor/Master Electrician exams, effective February 2021. You still have to complete the Board of Industrial Trades application.

How many hours do I need for a DC journeyman electrician license?

The DC Code's main route is at least 8,000 hours as an apprentice electrician over at least four years. An electrical engineering degree plus two years of field work certified by a master electrician also qualifies, and the Board can accept equivalent experience.

Is the NASCLA journeyman exam open book?

Yes. PSI's bulletin allows the listed references, and you may highlight them and add permanent tabs. Handwritten notes, loose papers and removable tabs are not allowed.

How many questions are on the NASCLA journeyman exam and how long is it?

PSI lists 100 scored questions plus 10 unscored pretest questions, with 300 minutes to finish. The published minimum passing score is 70.

How much does a DC journeyman license cost?

DLCP publishes a $65 application fee and a $110 journeyman license fee for a term of up to two years. NASCLA charges $65 to apply for the exam and $45 per transcript, and PSI charges its own exam fee.

Can I get a DC electrician license through reciprocity?

Yes, if you hold a Maryland or Virginia journeyman or master license that you earned by exam and that is current and in good standing. Upload a letter of good standing or license verification from your home board with your DC application.

Do I need a code class for the DC journeyman license?

Yes. The DC Code asks for a class on the District's electrical code, DCMR Title 12-C, or an equivalent code, completed within two years before you apply.

When do DC electrician licenses expire?

All DC electrician licenses expire on November 30 of odd-numbered years. The published journeyman renewal fee is $110, with a $50 late fee.

Does passing NASCLA give me a license in DC?

No. The NASCLA result covers the exam requirement only. The Board of Industrial Trades still reviews your experience, code class and documents before issuing a license.
